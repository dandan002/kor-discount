  {\normalsize\textbf{Abstract}}

  \begin{minipage}{0.85\textwidth}
    \normalsize\singlespacing
    South Korean equities have traded at persistent discounts to global peers for at least three decades,
    a phenomenon widely termed the \enquote{Korea Discount.}  This paper synthesizes the growing empirical
    and legal literature on the discount's measurement, causes, and remedies.  We organize the evidence
    around three competing—and often complementary—explanations: (i) corporate governance failures
    rooted in the chaebol ownership structure; (ii) structural weakness in corporate growth potential
    and capital allocation; and (iii) demand-side frictions, including short-termism among domestic
    investors and asymmetric foreign-investor discipline.  We further review recent policy responses,
    including the 2024 Corporate Value-Up Program and the 2025 Commercial Act amendment, and assess
    their expected efficacy in light of the empirical record.  The literature converges on the view that
    the discount is deeply structural: no single governance or payout reform is likely to close the gap
    without simultaneous improvement in long-run profitability and investment quality.
  \end{minipage}

\section{Introduction}
%%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The Korean stock market has long attracted attention for one of the most persistent valuation
anomalies in international finance: listed Korean firms systematically trade below book value and at
lower earnings multiples than comparably profitable firms in other economies, even after controlling
for standard risk factors.  This \enquote{Korea Discount} is not a fleeting market inefficiency.
\citet{jin2026} document that Korean firms exhibited statistically significant undervaluation across
a 48-country panel spanning 1990--2023, and that the discount persisted across all three sub-decades
examined.  \citet{choi2012} reach a similar conclusion using cost-of-equity capital comparisons
across 31 countries: Korean firms bear a cost of equity significantly above what firm characteristics
would predict, though the gap had narrowed somewhat by the early 2010s.

The Korea Discount is consequential beyond academic finance.  A chronically undervalued equity market
raises the cost of external financing, depresses household wealth, discourages domestic investment,
and has become a focal point of economic policy.  In 2024, the Korean Financial Services Commission
launched a \enquote{Corporate Value-Up Program} explicitly modeled on Japan's \textit{Tokyo Stock
Exchange PBR Improvement Initiative}, and the National Assembly subsequently passed a landmark
amendment to the Commercial Act in July 2025 to strengthen minority shareholder protections.

Yet the diagnosis remains contested.  Attribution of the discount to governance failures, growth
weakness, or behavioral frictions has direct implications for what kinds of policy will work.  This
review systematically examines the empirical literature to assess which explanations have the
strongest support and what the evidence implies for the effectiveness of recent reforms.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:measurement} reviews the
measurement of the Korea Discount and establishes its magnitude and persistence.  Section~\ref{sec:causes}
surveys the three main causal hypotheses: governance (Section~\ref{subsec:governance}), growth
and capital allocation (Section~\ref{subsec:growth}), and demand-side frictions
(Section~\ref{subsec:demand}).  Section~\ref{sec:policy} evaluates the policy responses.
Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} synthesizes the evidence and identifies open questions.

\section{Measuring the Korea Discount}
\label{sec:measurement}
%%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The Korea Discount is conventionally measured as the gap between the price-to-book ratio (PBR) or
price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of the Korean market (proxied by the KOSPI or KOSPI\,200 indices) and
that of a relevant peer group—typically developed Asian markets, the MSCI Emerging Markets index,
or a cross-country regression-predicted benchmark.

\citet{jin2026} provide the most comprehensive long-run evidence to date.  Using data for 48 countries
over 1990--2023, they confirm that Korean firms' P/B and P/E ratios consistently ranked among the
lowest globally, and that this undervaluation remained statistically significant after controlling for
firm size, profitability, and growth, as well as country-level GDP per capita, financial market
development, and geopolitical risk.  Critically, the discount was not confined to specific business
cycles or crisis periods: subsample analyses covering the 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s all yield the
same conclusion, establishing that the Korea Discount is a structural, three-decade phenomenon
rather than a cyclical aberration.

\citet{jung2022} provide complementary within-sample evidence.  The historical average book-to-market
(BTM) ratio of Korean listed companies exceeds one, meaning that the market systematically values
Korean firms below their accounting net assets.  Using firm-level data from 1995 to 2019, they confirm
that high-BTM Korean stocks deliver higher subsequent returns, consistent with mispricing rather than
rational risk compensation: the return pattern is asymmetric toward positive extremes, a signature of
systematic undervaluation rather than elevated systematic risk.

\citet{choi2012} approach measurement from the cost of equity capital side.  Analyzing analysts'
earnings forecasts to back out implied equity premiums for firms in 32 countries, they find that
Korean companies face a significantly higher cost of equity than comparable foreign peers, consistent
with investors demanding a discount for Korea-specific risks.  This framing connects the valuation gap
directly to the capital market implications: the Korea Discount translates into a structural financing
disadvantage for Korean firms.

In summary, the literature establishes that the Korea Discount is large (Korean P/B ratios frequently
run 30--50\% below peer markets on an adjusted basis), persistent (three decades of data), and
pervasive (present across firm sizes, industries, and business cycles).  The question is not whether
the discount exists, but why.

\section{Causes of the Korea Discount}
\label{sec:causes}
%%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Corporate Governance and the Chaebol Structure}
\label{subsec:governance}

The dominant popular explanation for the Korea Discount centers on corporate governance failures
at chaebol conglomerates.  \citet{economist2012} offers a succinct articulation: if controlling
shareholders can extract private benefits at the expense of minority investors—through \enquote{tunnelling}
(awarding overpriced contracts to family-owned affiliates) and \enquote{propping} (subsidizing
failing sister companies)—rational outside investors will discount expected future cash flows,
depressing valuations.  The geopolitical explanation—that proximity to North Korea depresses Korean
valuations—is explicitly addressed and dismissed: the KOSPI quickly recovered after the December 2011
death of Kim Jong Il, and geopolitical controls do not eliminate the discount in cross-country
regressions \citep{jin2026}.

\citet{njoku2026} examine one specific governance mechanism—dividend policy—in a setting that allows
them to disentangle chaebol and non-chaebol firms.  Using a panel of 558 KOSPI-listed non-financial
firms over 2011--2019, they find that governance quality (measured by Korea Corporate Governance
Service scores) is positively associated with dividends for non-chaebol firms across all governance
dimensions.  For chaebol-affiliated firms, however, this relationship is \textit{selective}: only
shareholder rights protection retains independent explanatory power, while board structure, audit
committee quality, and disclosure transparency do not.  This finding is important because it shows
that concentrated ownership does not merely attenuate governance effects—it selectively nullifies
some mechanisms while preserving others.  The policy implication is that broad governance score
improvements may be ineffective in chaebol firms unless they specifically target minority shareholder
rights.

\citet{kang2026} test governance explanations using a natural experiment: they examine abnormal
returns around two events—the June 2025 presidential election and the July 2025 passage of the
Commercial Act amendment—for firms classified as high-BTM (Korea Discount risk group) versus
others.  Firms in the highest BTM tercile exhibit significantly positive abnormal returns at both
event windows, with larger effects at the parliamentary passage date, suggesting the market interprets
the governance reform as materially reducing the probability of minority shareholder expropriation.
Importantly, no differential reaction is observed among firms with high controlling shareholder
ownership, consistent with the view that the market does not expect governance reforms to bind
equally across all ownership structures.

\citet{park2025} examines a related structural feature: the use of cross-shareholdings among chaebol
affiliates as a mechanism for entrenching management control.  Cross-shareholding structures
enable controlling families to maintain voting control well in excess of their economic stakes,
limiting the disciplinary force of capital markets.  \citet{park2025} argues that the threat of
affiliate-driven boardroom coups using voting restriction strategies could, paradoxically, incentivize
holding company revaluation—a channel through which the Korea Discount might narrow even without
direct governance legislation.

The governance hypothesis receives strong support overall, but several papers raise important nuances.
\citet{kim2025} argue that measured shareholder rights scores do not, on average, rank Korea lower
than comparable countries, questioning the premise that Korea has weaker de jure investor protections.
\citet{yang2025} go further: in a direct horse-race between governance, shareholder payout, and
growth potential variables in explaining cross-sectional PBR variation among Korean firms, governance
variables exhibit no statistically significant relationship with PBR.  These findings suggest that
the governance mechanism—if operative—works through subtle de facto channels (selective compliance,
concentrated ownership, tunnelling) rather than through measurable governance score improvements,
and that governance alone is unlikely to explain the full magnitude of the discount.

\subsection{Growth Potential and Capital Allocation}
\label{subsec:growth}

A second strand of research attributes the Korea Discount primarily to weak corporate growth
prospects and inefficient capital allocation rather than governance failures per se.

\citet{yang2025} provide the clearest empirical case.  Using cross-sectional regressions of PBR on
governance, payout, and growth variables, they find that growth-related variables dominate: firms
with low R\&D expenditure, higher tangible asset intensity (plants and land relative to intangible
capital such as patents and brand value), and older firms beyond peak growth consistently display
lower PBRs.  The implication is that the Korean market's low valuations primarily reflect investors'
rational assessment of weak future growth, not expropriation risk.

\citet{lee2025pbr} adopts a capital allocation lens.  Modeling both short-run and long-run
determinants of PBR changes, he finds that cash retention, R\&D investment, general capital
expenditure, and dividends all have positive long-run effects on PBR for the overall firm sample.
However, for large manufacturing firms—the backbone of the Korean economy—dividend expansion has
\textit{weak or negative} effects, while R\&D investment is the dominant value driver.  This finding
directly challenges policies focused on dividend increases as the primary lever for PBR improvement:
for the firms that matter most to the KOSPI's aggregate valuation, what creates value is investment
quality, not payout generosity.

\citet{jin2026} corroborate this view from a cross-country perspective.  Among OECD countries that
achieved significant long-run multiple expansion, the key factors were profitability improvement,
appropriate liquidity management, and financial market development.  By contrast, higher shareholder
return ratios, short-term trading activity, and elevated geopolitical risk were negatively associated
with valuation improvements.  This evidence directly contradicts simple payout-based explanations
of the Korea Discount and suggests that durable multiple expansion requires building profitability
foundations over years or decades.

\citet{kim2025} offer additional evidence for a structural demand explanation rooted in investor
behavior.  Korean firms' trading turnover ranked 3rd among 58 countries in their sample, yet the
degree to which corporate fundamentals—profitability and growth—were reflected in valuations ranked
51st.  The predictive power of market-to-book ratios for future earnings was significantly weaker
in Korea than in other markets, suggesting that high short-term trading activity crowds out
long-horizon price discovery.  In this view, the Korea Discount is partly endogenous to investor
behavior: a short-term trading culture prevents long-run corporate fundamentals from being capitalized
into equity prices, creating a self-reinforcing undervaluation trap.

\subsection{Demand-Side Frictions: Investor Behavior and Information}
\label{subsec:demand}

A third cluster of explanations focuses on demand-side frictions—features of the investor base,
information environment, or market microstructure that suppress Korean equity valuations
independently of firm-level fundamentals.

\citet{yun2022} examine how investor sentiment affects audit quality in the Korean setting.  They
find that initial audit fees are significantly lower when investor sentiment is high, and that
Big\,4 and specialist auditors input fewer audit hours during high-sentiment periods.  While this
paper is not directly about the Korea Discount, it illuminates a broader principle: when sentiment
drives trading activity, the market's ability to discipline managers and detect opportunistic behavior
weakens, which should rationally lead to lower valuations once sentiment reverts.  A market prone to
sentiment-driven overconfidence may price-in discount premiums to offset expected future audit-quality
failures.

\citet{joo2026} examine foreign investors as a disciplining mechanism.  Standard OLS regressions
suggest that foreign ownership is positively correlated with growth disparity (the gap between
sustainable and actual growth rates), but this reflects selection: foreign investors prefer
growth-oriented firms.  After addressing endogeneity through propensity score matching, foreign
ownership \textit{reduces} growth disparity significantly, correcting over-investment in over-growing
firms and encouraging profitable management in under-growing ones.  Foreign investors thus function
as smart monitors rather than short-term speculators.  The corollary is that policies discouraging
or restricting foreign investment—or a market environment perceived as hostile to foreign capital—
would deprive Korean firms of an important governance mechanism, exacerbating the discount.

The equity premium evidence in \citet{choi2012} is also relevant here.  A higher cost of equity
for Korean firms can reflect not only governance or growth concerns, but also liquidity premia,
information asymmetry premiums, and limited investor base.  Market development—breadth of the
institutional investor base, disclosure quality, analyst coverage—affects the equilibrium price
level independent of firm fundamentals.

\section{Policy Responses}
\label{sec:policy}
%%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The persistence of the Korea Discount has motivated a succession of policy responses, each informed
by a particular causal diagnosis.

\subsection{The Corporate Value-Up Program (2024)}

In February 2024, Korea's Financial Services Commission launched the Corporate Value-Up Program,
modeled closely on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's 2023 initiative requiring firms trading below book
value to disclose improvement plans.  \citet{lee2025vup} provides a comprehensive legal and policy
assessment.  She notes that, unlike Japan's experience—where the disclosure requirement triggered
substantial corporate action and a multi-year bull market—the initial Korean program was largely
ignored: most KOSPI and KOSDAQ firms opted out, and the index closed weaker at year-end 2024.

Several structural differences likely explain the divergent outcomes.  Japan's program was
accompanied by sustained shareholder activism, a favorable macroeconomic environment, and cross-
shareholding unwind incentives from the Bank of Japan's equity holdings.  Korea's program, by
contrast, was voluntary with no enforcement mechanism, and was introduced into a period of domestic
political uncertainty.  \citet{lee2025pbr} further cautions that dividend-centric interpretations
of the value-up program may be counterproductive for large manufacturers, where R\&D investment
rather than payout expansion drives PBR improvement.

\subsection{Commercial Act Amendment (2025)}

The July 2025 amendment to Korea's Commercial Act addressed several long-standing governance
weaknesses: expanded fiduciary duties to include minority shareholders, tightened related-party
transaction approvals, and enhanced board independence requirements.  \citet{kang2026} find that
high-BTM firms earned significantly positive abnormal returns around both the election event (which
signaled reform probability) and the legislative passage date, with larger market reactions at the
formal enactment.  This event-study evidence is the most direct empirical test that governance
reform matters for the Korea Discount, and the results are encouraging.

However, \citet{kang2026} also find that firms with high controlling shareholder ownership did not
exhibit differential market reactions, suggesting the market discounts the amendment's ability to
constrain controlling families.  This is consistent with \citet{njoku2026}'s finding that governance
improvements in chaebol firms flow primarily through shareholder rights channels, not through
structural board reforms.

\subsection{Implications of the Causal Evidence for Policy}

The empirical literature provides a nuanced guide for policy design.  If the Korea Discount primarily
reflects weak growth potential—as \citet{yang2025} and \citet{jin2026} argue—then governance and
payout reforms will have limited effects.  The growth diagnosis calls for industrial and innovation
policy: measures that increase R\&D intensity, facilitate entry and exit, reduce zombie firm prevalence
(identified as a persistent drag by \citealt{lee2025vup}), and develop the long-term institutional
investor base that can fund growth-oriented businesses.

If short-termism among domestic investors is a root cause—as \citet{kim2025} suggest—then market
microstructure reforms (transaction taxes, holding period incentives, institutional investor
development) may be more effective than corporate disclosure mandates.  The finding of \citet{joo2026}
that foreign investors correct growth disparity points toward policies that lower barriers to
foreign portfolio investment and improve English-language disclosure.

If governance remains an independent cause despite skepticism from \citet{yang2025}, the evidence
from \citet{njoku2026} and \citet{kang2026} suggests reforms should focus specifically on minority
shareholder rights rather than broad governance score metrics.  In particular, related-party
transaction controls and cumulative voting rights—tools that reduce tunnelling and propping—may
be more effective than board composition requirements, which chaebol firms can satisfy formally
without substantive effect.

\section{Synthesis and Research Gaps}
\label{sec:synthesis}
%%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The literature reviewed here converges on several important conclusions.  First, the Korea Discount
is a structural, multi-decade phenomenon that cannot be attributed to any single factor.  Its
persistence across business cycles and its robustness to standard controls for risk and fundamentals
imply that it reflects deep institutional and behavioral features of the Korean economy.

Second, governance and growth explanations are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.  Chaebol
governance failures reduce the share of firm value that accrues to minority shareholders, while weak
growth prospects reduce total value.  The relative magnitude of each contribution likely varies across
firm groups: governance effects appear stronger among non-chaebol firms and firms with diffuse
ownership (consistent with \citealt{njoku2026} and \citealt{kang2026}), while growth and investment
effects dominate for large manufacturing conglomerates (consistent with \citealt{yang2025} and
\citealt{lee2025pbr}).

Third, demand-side frictions amplify supply-side weaknesses.  Short-termism among domestic investors
prevents long-run corporate value from being incorporated in prices, creating an environment in which
even well-governed, high-growth firms may be undervalued.  Foreign investor activism partially
mitigates this (per \citealt{joo2026}), but only for firms foreign capital finds accessible.

Fourth, recent policy interventions show early promise but face structural headwinds.  The
Commercial Act amendment produced measurably positive market reactions for high-BTM firms
\citep{kang2026}, but governance reform is unlikely to be sufficient on its own.  The value-up
program's initial underperformance relative to Japan's experience suggests that voluntary
disclosure requirements without enforcement mechanisms are insufficient in a concentrated ownership
environment.

Several important gaps remain.  The literature lacks rigorous causal identification of the long-run
effect of specific governance reforms on Korean equity valuations; most evidence is event-study based
or cross-sectional.  The interaction between macroeconomic policy uncertainty—identified by
\citet{kim2025} as an understudied driver—and the Korea Discount merits further structural modeling.
The literature on foreign investor discipline \citep{joo2026} relies on KOSPI and KOSDAQ data
through 2024 and does not yet capture the 2025 post-reform period.  Finally, comparisons with Japan's
value-up experience remain preliminary; a systematic comparative study exploiting the near-simultaneous
policy adoptions in both markets would substantially advance the literature.
