\begin{abstract}
South Korean equities have traded at a persistent structural discount to developed and emerging market peers for at least three decades. This paper synthesizes the academic literature on the ``Korea Discount,'' drawing on 26 studies to assess the competing explanations---concentrated Chaebol ownership and tunneling, weak minority shareholder protections, and stagnant corporate growth prospects---and to evaluate the reform mechanisms proposed to resolve it. We find that while growth fundamentals and governance deficits are jointly implicated, the causal evidence from natural experiments consistently identifies the self-dealing channel as a direct and quantifiable source of value destruction. Institutional stewardship through the National Pension Service and the Stewardship Code has produced limited and conditional improvements, insufficient on their own to close the valuation gap. We support this conclusion with an original empirical contribution: a 20-year monthly panel of index-level price-to-book (P/B) ratios comparing Korea to Japan as a governance reform benchmark. Our results show that Korea's relative valuation penalty deepened precisely during the period when Japan escalated from voluntary codes to mandatory, exchange-level structural enforcement in 2023, while Korea remained on a voluntary program. The evidence points to a consistent conclusion across the literature and our own analysis: mitigating the Korea Discount requires hard legislative reform---specifically, amendments to the Commercial Act mandating broader fiduciary duties---rather than continued reliance on soft-law guidelines.
\end{abstract}
