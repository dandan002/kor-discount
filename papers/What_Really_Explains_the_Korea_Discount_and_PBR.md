# What Really Explains the Korea Discount and PBR? 
## A Comparison of the Growth Potential, Shareholder Payout, and Governance Hypotheses

**Cheol-Won Yang**, Professor, Dankook University  
**Shu-Feng Wang**, Associate Professor, Ajou University  
**Jaewon Choi**, Professor, Seoul National University

### Abstract
This study investigates which factors - growth potential, shareholder payout, or corporate governance—explain the persistently low price-to-book ratios(PBRs) of Korean firms. We find that corporate governance has no significant relationship with PBR, and firms with higher shareholder payout tendencies tend to have lower PBRs. This contradicts prevailing hypotheses that associate shareholder payout and governance improvements with higher valuations.

In contrast, variables linked to corporate growth potential exhibit a strong correlation with PBR. Firms with low research and development (R&D) expenditures and investments, those with a higher proportion of tangible assets (e.g., plants and land) rather than intangible capital (e.g., patents and brand value), and mature firms beyond their growth phase tend to have lower PBRs. These findings suggest that the low PBR of Korean firms is primarily stem from weak growth prospects. A low PBR has traditionally been associated with high stock returns due to the value premium effect. However, this effect, both in Korea and globally, declined sharply following the 2008 global financial crisis. Therefore, the low PBR and stock returns of Korean firms can be interpreted as a consequence of the disappearance of the value premium in an environment of diminishing corporate growth potential.

**Keywords:** PBR, Corporate governance, Shareholder payout, Growth potential, Value premium  
**JEL Classification:** G18, G35

## 1. Introduction

Recently, the Korean capital market has been heated with discussions on value-up policies to resolve the Korea discount. The logic of the Korea discount is that the PBR of Korean companies is excessively low compared to major countries, setting the core indicator as the Price-to-Book Ratio (PBR). The focus of the policy is on how to increase PBR.

Studies arguing for the Korea discount mainly conduct cross-country comparisons to support the existence of the discount phenomenon and pinpoint its causes. First, they present the fact that Korea's PBR is lower than that of major countries to show the existence of the Korea discount phenomenon, and then try to find the cause by identifying key variables that determine differences in PBR among countries. Previous studies suggest poor corporate governance or shareholder return policies in Korea, and insufficient investor protection systems as causes. Eventually, it concludes with the logic that corporate governance in Korea must be improved and shareholder return policies strengthened to improve PBR.

This study re-examines the approaches and results of previous papers more strictly and critically from two perspectives. First, existing cross-country comparative studies focus on average PBR by country, so they cannot sufficiently reflect the heterogeneity of industrial structures and corporate characteristics. As a result, it is difficult to accurately answer the question, "What explains the PBR of an individual company?" In a situation where industrial structures and corporate characteristics differ among countries, it is difficult to identify the cause of the Korea discount simply by comparing averages. Therefore, this study attempts to examine the determinants of PBR by conducting a cross-sectional analysis at the corporate level targeting individual Korean companies. That is, assuming the results of previous literature that 'Korea's average PBR is lower than other countries', we examine what explains that low PBR more strictly and critically with corporate-level data.

Second is a fundamental question about using the PBR indicator as a policy goal. This is because academically there is still debate and no consensus has been reached on what PBR represents (i.e., whether it is a signal of undervaluation or an indicator of growth potential). This issue is related to the discussion on the value premium. Most financial studies in the 1970s-90s support the existence of a value premium, where a low PBR brings high returns in many countries. A low PBR is a signal that the company is undervalued or high-risk, so investing in these value stocks will yield high returns in the future. In practice, investors such as Graham and Warren Buffett have led value stock investments and proven that investing in low PBR stocks can yield high long-term performance.

The existence of a value premium has two implications. First, it implies that the low PBR of Korean companies is not a problem for stock investors. The logic of the Korea discount views low PBR as leading to low returns, causing investor losses, but this contradicts mainstream empirical results in finance literature. Low PBR tends to be linked to high future returns, so it can actually be a positive investment signal. Second, therefore, policy efforts to raise PBR have weak validity as a policy goal and require re-examination.

Then why does the phenomenon of low PBR leading to low returns occur? To answer this question, this study (1) first analyzes the determinants of PBR, and then (2) analyzes the current state of the value premium in the Korean stock market and global markets in the latter half. In conclusion, Korea's low PBR phenomenon is due to the weakening of corporate growth potential, and the disappearance of the value premium is observed in corporate groups with weak growth potential, leading to low stock returns. That is, weakening growth potential → PBR decline, and alienation of value stocks (low PBR stocks) in the global growth stock investment boom → resulting in lower returns.

The main results of this study are as follows. First, payout ratio shows a significant negative (-) relationship with PBR. That is, companies with higher payout ratios have lower PBR. Considering that low PBR is typically pointed out as the cause of the Korea discount, this is an unexpected and surprising result. However, considering the growth stock boom that has led global stock markets since the global financial crisis, it is a natural result.
