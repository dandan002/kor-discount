ABSTRACT The historical average book-to-market ratio (BTM) of Korean listed companies is above one, and often used as evidence for the Korea
discount. This paper asks whether the BTM reflects either risk inherent in Korean businesses as claimed in the popular press, or a bias in market
expectations. Analyzing data of Korean listed firms from 1995 to 2019, we first confirm that stocks with BTM above one consistently outperform
the rest, consistent with the empirical findings documented in prior studies. When we compose three summary indicators that are allegedly associated
with the Korea discount (i.e., fundamental factor, geopolitical factor, and governance factor), we find that only governance factor is positively related
to BTM as alluded to in the business press. In our subsequent analyses borrowed from Ohlson and Bilinski’s (2015) specification, we find that
BTM above one is associated with a higher probability of positive extreme returns, but a lower probability of negative extreme returns. The finding
indicates that BTM above one is more likely to reflect characteristics associated with stock mispricing. We further explore why such systematic
bias persists over time and report some evidence that governance factor plays a crucial role. This paper highlights the importance of recasting the
underlying cause of a phenomenon.

KEYWORDS book-to-market ratio, Korea discount, risk explanation, mispricing explanation

Ⅰ. Introduction
Korean stocks are traded at a discount (a.k.a., the Korea discount). The financial press
reports that 1) the price-to-book ratios (an inverse of the book-to-market ratios) are on
average below one in Korea and 2) are lower than those in other countries.1) Potential
conduits include the fundamental risk inherent in an industrial structure (e.g., The Economist
2012; Yoon 2020), geopolitical risk (e.g., Kim and Park 2019; Jung et al. 2021; Pyo 2021),
and governance risk (Forbes 2001, 2007; Rhee 2018; Ducret and Isakov 2020). It is unclear,
however, whether and to what extent the book-to-market ratio (BTM hereafter) reflects the
risk as claimed in the press (i.e., the risk explanation) because it could also manifest a bias
in market expectation (i.e., the mispricing explanation). Given that the academic literature

does not converge on this issue (e.g., Ali et al. 2003), we assess the validity of relying on
BTM in suggesting the widely accepted risk explanation for the Korea discount.
Analyzing data of Korean listed firms from 1995 to 2019, we first confirm that stocks
with BTM above one consistently outperform the rest, consistent with the empirical findings
documented in prior studies. Interestingly, when we compose three summary indicators that
are allegedly associated with the Korea discount (i.e., fundamental factor, geopolitical factor,
and governance factor), we find that only governance factor is positively related to BTM as
alluded to in the business press.
More importantly, subsequent analyses based on Ohlson and Bilinski (2015) reveal that
BTM above one is associated with a higher probability of positive extreme returns, but a
lower probability of negative extreme returns. This finding contradicts the risk explanation
that would predict higher probabilities for both positive and negative extreme returns, but
instead supports the mispricing explanation.
We further explore why such systematic bias emerges and persists over time. We find that
the mispricing captured in the Ohlson and Bilinski (2015) model is more salient in firms
with higher governance factor. These results suggest that governance factor hinders market
participants from correcting bias in a timely manner. In contrast, we find little evidence that
either fundamental factor or geopolitical factor induces mispricing.
Our paper provides important contributions to the practice. First, we highlight the
importance of recasting the underlying cause of a phenomenon. Practitioners have long
assumed that BTM reflects the Korea discount. When we horserace two competing
explanations (i.e., risk or mispricing), however, we find evidence supportive of the mispricing
explanation that BTM reflects market failures of correctly assessing risk profiles of Korean
companies. Second and more importantly, our findings have policy implications. Our results
suggest that governance is indeed of a primary concern for the Korea discount. Our results
would help set a priority of policy means to mitigate the Korea discount.
Our discussion also adds to the academic literature related to the return predictability of
BTM. First, we extend the related literature by explicitly documenting the existence of the
BTM effect in the Korean stock market. Second, we discuss the validity of BTM measures
to describe Korean discount. Doing so, we belong to a rare stream of literature discussing on

Ⅱ. Literature Review and Research Question
Numerous studies in finance document a positive association between the BTM and
subsequent

stock

returns

(e.g.,

Fama

and French

1992;

1993),

with two competing

explanations. On the one hand, the BTM reflects a risk for which investors demand
compensation. Fama and French (1992, 1993) argue that the return on the BTM compensates
for the risk related to economic fundamentals, noting that high BTM stocks tend to have low
earnings-to-assets ratios and low earnings persistence. Subsequent studies attempt to figure out the
substance of risk reflected in the BTM, namely operating/financing risk (Penman et al. 2007),
risk in earnings growth (Penman and Reggiani 2013), risk contained in the retained earnings
(Ball et al. 2020), or macroeconomic risk (Petkova and Zhang 2005; Barth et al. 2021).
On the other hand, another stream of literature argues that the BTM-return association
stems from a systematic mispricing of stocks with extreme BTM. They show that market
participants underestimate future earnings for high BTM stocks and overestimate future
earnings for low BTM stocks. Such systematically biased expectations are gradually corrected,
leading to superior subsequent returns for high BTM stocks (La Porta et al. 1997; Skinner
and Sloan 2002; Ali et al. 2003). Related studies suggest that arbitrage costs (Shleifer and
Vishny 1997; Ali et al. 2003), investor sophistication (Lakonishok et al. 1994), or transaction
costs (Knez and Ready 1996; Barber et al. 2001) explain why the systematic mispricing is
not quickly and completely traded away.
Collectively, it is hard to conclude whether any noted factor related to the BTM is a risk,
not only because the specified asset pricing model in Fama and French (1993, 1996) is based
on an empirical analysis rather than on theory, but also because the observed BTM-return

in this assumption.

Ⅲ. Data and Descriptive Statistics
3.1 Data and Sample
Our data includes Korean listed firms for the period from 1995 to 2019. From the initial
sample, we exclude financial institutions and require that all variables are available for the
analyses. We restrict the sample to firms with a December year-end. We also exclude
firm-year observations with a negative book value. We obtain financial and stock market
information from DataGuide. Ownership and governance information is obtained from the
TS2000 database. The sources of other information used in analyses are indicated in the
relevant part of the paper. The final sample comprises 30,712 firm-year observations.2)

3.2 Descriptive Statistics
Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for the variables used in our analyses. The
variable definitions are provided in the Appendix. The sample mean (median) of the
book-to-market ratio (BTM) defined as the book value of equity divided by the market value
of equity is 1.390 (1.063), confirming that Korean stocks are on average traded at a discount
as featured in the financial press.

that the medians of BTM generally exceed one before the mid-2000s and gradually decrease
following the peak in 2008. The portion of BTM>1 shows similar patterns. However, almost
50 percent of each yearly sample has a BTM above one, which is considerably higher than
the percentage reported in the U.S. sample (Barth et al. 2021). Collectively, Figure 1 confirms

In Equation (1), we include the various determinants of BTM following prior literature
(e.g., Barth et al. 2021; Cho et al. 2021). First, following Cho et al. (2021), we include a
dummy variable that indicates the adoption of the International Financial Reporting Standard
(IFRS) to control the effect of accounting standards. If the findings of Cho et al. (2021) that
the gap between stock price and firm value has narrowed after the adoption of IFRS could
be applied to book-to-market ratios as well, we expect a negative sign on α2. Next, we
include several firm-level characteristics known to affect the book-to-market ratios (Barth et
al. 2021). We control for cash and cash equivalents (CASH), net accounts receivables
(RECEIVABLE), total inventory (INVENTORY), fixed assets (PPENT), total intangible assets
(INTANGIBLES), the amount of goodwill (GOODWILL), total amount of R&D costs
(RD_CAPITALIZED and RD_EXPENSED) and total liabilities (LIABILITY). To control the
effect of other types of assets, we include OTHERASSETS which indicates assets other than
those controlled above. All firm-level variables are scaled by the market value of equity at
the beginning of the year (Barth et al. 2021).

4.2 Does BTM>1 outperform BTM≤1?
In this section, we examine whether BTM is related with subsequent stock returns as
documented in the prior literature (e.g., Fama and French 1992, 1993). We specifically
compare the future stock returns of BTM>1 with those of BTM≤1. We measure future stock
returns as the average monthly return over the next twelve months starting six months after
the fiscal year-end (ANNRET). In column (1) of Panel A in Table 3, the average ANNRET
for BTM>1 is 0.021, meaning that firms with BTM above one report stock returns of 2.1%
in the next twelve months on average. More importantly, such performance is superior to that
for BTM≤1, which averages 0.007. The difference in mean values between these two groups
is significant (t-stat=19.20, p-value<0.001) in general. However, the difference narrows over

time as presented in columns (2) to (6). The narrowing difference may suggest either that: 1)
the risk premium on BTM decreases due to structural changes in risk profiles, or 2) the
mispricing associated with BTM diminishes over time possibly due to arbitragers.
In Panel B, we further examine whether the return difference varies with FFACTOR,
GPFACTOR, and GOVFACTOR. Specifically, we divide the sample into two groups depending
on whether each factor is above the sample median. We find that the return difference
between stocks with BTM>1 and those with BTM≤1 is significantly higher for firms with
higher FFACTOR or higher GOVFACTOR. These findings suggest that BTM>1 reflects both
fundamental factor and governance factor and can be compensated for in subsequent stock
returns. In stark contrast, however, the return difference is greater when GPFACTOR is lower.
This is inconsistent with the risk explanation. Collectively, the results in Table 3 suggest that
both FFACTOR and GOVFACTOR can be potential conduits for the Korea discount.

4.3 Risk or mispricing?
As aforementioned, two explanations exist for the BTM-return relations in prior studies. To
distinguish which explanation is a better determinant of the Korea discount, we rely on the
methodology developed in Ohlson and Bilinski (2015), which focuses on the signs of
correlations between a trading attribute and extreme (i.e., high and low) returns. If the sign
of the correlation coefficient between an attribute and a high return is opposite to that of a
low return, it is more likely that the attribute-return relation is due to mispricing.
Alternatively, if two correlations share the same sign, the risk explanation is more plausible.
To follow this approach, we estimate the following two logit regressions:
P(HIGHABRET) = α1 + α2 VOL + α3 BETA + α4 LOG(MVE)
+ α5 LOG(BTM) +Year/Industry dummies +ε.

(2)

P(LOWABRET) = α1 + α2 VOL + α3 BETA + α4 LOG(MVE)
+ α5 LOG(BTM) +Year/Industry dummies +ε.

(3)

months cumulative market-adjusted return starting six months after the fiscal year end
(CUMABRET) is higher (lower) than 50 (-50) percent. We include a set of risk controls,
such as return volatility (VOL), market beta (BETA), and firm market capitalization
(LOG(MVE)). The variable of interest is LOG(BTM). LOG(BTM) is the natural log of BTM.

Ⅴ. Conclusion
The financial press continues to use BTM>1 as supporting evidence for the Korea discount.
We test the validity of doing so. The novel approach of this paper is that we first construct
three summary indicators for the factors allegedly reflective of the profiles of Korean
businesses, namely fundamental factor, geopolitical factor, and governance factor. We then
link these variables to BTM>1 and find that only the governance factor is positively
associated with BTM>1. Next, we ask whether the positive return predictability of BTM>1 is
due to risk or mispricing. Our analyses based on Ohlson and Bilinski (2015) confirm that the
return predictability of BTM>1 is more likely due to mispricing than to risk. We also find
evidence that governance factor is mainly responsible for this result. We conclude that
governance factor primarily hinders market participants from correcting biases reflected in the
BTM.
We hasten to point out that this study is by no means the ultimate answer to whether the
Korea discount is due to risk or mispricing. Rather, with caution, our findings imply that the
Korea discount is at least partly explained by the mispricing associated with the BTM.
Throughout the analyses, we find that governance factor consistently plays a nontrivial role in
explaining both the BTM and the return predictability of the BTM. By extension, our
findings suggest that regulators and policy makers may be well-placed to leverage corporate
