ABSTRACT

KEYWORDS

This study examines whether high sentiment affects auditors’ risk assessment in a less litigious
environment. Specifically, we use a Korean setting with relatively low litigation risks and investigate
whether audit fees are lower when firms change auditors during high sentiment times. Lower audit
fees responding to high sentiment in a weak legal environment may indicate that auditors perceive
the overall audit risk including reputation risk and regulation risk as low within the given period,
increasing the possibility of their failure to detect managerial opportunistic behaviour. Using
17,469 Korean public firm-year observations from 2003 to 2017, we find that initial audit fees are
significantly lower when investor sentiment is high, while changing auditors has no influence on
audit fees when investor sentiment is low. Furthermore, this result is maintained even when
auditors are changed to Big4 or industry specialist auditors (SPEC), which are known to have
high ability and risk sensitivity. We also find that Big4 and SPEC initial auditors input significantly
less audit hours during high investor sentiment periods, suggesting that optimistic bias caused by
high sentiment makes auditors expect that relaxed audits are tolerable regardless of the legal
environment.

I. Introduction

We analyse the effect of investor sentiment on
external auditors’ risk assessments, measured by
initial engagement discounts. The accounting lit­
erature assumes that investors are rational, but
recent studies show that investor sentiment,1
a cognitive bias, can cause deviations from the
fundamental values of stock prices (Baker and
Wurgler 2006). Social recognition argues that opti­
mistic individuals pay limited attention to informa­
tion, while pessimistic individuals make a thorough
investigation (Bless et al. 1996). Consistent with
this, investors in high sentiment periods respond
more to unexpected positive earnings and over­
value stock prices (Baker and Wurgler 2006; Seok,
Cho, and Ryu 2019). Managers and analysts oppor­
tunistically use this situation by providing optimis­
tic forecasts during high sentiment times (Hurwitz
2018; Walther and Willis 2013). Given that inves­
tors use accounting variables in their investment
decisions and earnings are value relevant in high

If auditors recognize that high sentiment is asso­
ciated with the distortion of financial statements
leading to future lawsuits, they will increase the
scope of the audit and spend more audit hours to
ensure that financial statements are free of material
misstatements (professional duty view). However,
because high sentiment attracts investors to be
insensitive to financial statements, auditors are
likely to reduce their audit effort in the belief that
litigation risk due to financial misreporting will be
low in high sentiment times (commercial incen­
tives). Amin, Eshleman, and Guo (2021a) examine
two contrasting views, auditors’ professional duties
and commercial incentives,3 and find that audit
fees are lower on average in high sentiment peri­
ods, consistent with commercial incentives.
The findings of prior studies are based on the US
setting, where institutional investors are dominant
in securities markets and the legal environment is
strong; thus, high litigation risk sensitivity can
affect auditor behaviour. Therefore, the effect of
high sentiment on auditors needs to be examined
by using different legal environments. In this study,
we use a Korean setting with less developed stock
markets and a weak legal environment. This is
important because it can offer regulatory policy
implications for auditors in less litigious countries.
Although the most serious risk of threatening audi­
tors is litigation risk, it is not clear whether the legal
system affects prior findings because auditors are
influenced by the state of their mood (Cianci and
Bierstaker 2009).
The Korean stock market is ‘characterized by
significant information asymmetry among investor
types, high market sentiment, and prevalent inves­
tor psychology’ (Ryu, Kim, and Yang 2017, 826).
The litigation against auditors has increased since
class action lawsuits became effective in 2005, but
the litigation risk is relatively low compared to the

In particular, we focus on auditor changes, creat­

The results of Amin, Eshleman, and Guo (2021b)
show that high audit fees for risky firms, which
change auditors for favourable internal control
opinion in high sentiment periods, do not signifi­
cantly reduce the likelihood of restatements.
Practitioners and academics are interested in the
existence of initial discounts because it can lead to
less independent auditors (Cohen Commission
1978; DeAngelo 1981; Dye 1991). Korean public
or sizable companies are required to be audited by
the External Audit Act, so they do not value audits
highly, which leads to low audit fees on average.4 In
this environment, lower audit fees decrease audi­
tors’ incentives to input more effort in auditing,
increasing potential audit failure. Therefore, if
auditor changes in high sentiment periods are
related to lower audit fees, potential problems are
likely to be poorly identified and monitored by
auditors.

influence on audit fees in low investor sentiment
periods. Furthermore, this result is maintained
even when auditors are changed to Big4 or industry
specialist auditors (SPEC), which are known to
have high ability and risk sensitivity. This shows
that high sentiment influences auditor behaviour in

This study contributes to the literature in several
ways. First, we extend the literature on investor

sensitive to the legal environment. Our results of
accepting lower audit fees even in auditor change,
which requires more audit efforts to obtain reason­
able assurance about financial statements due to
information risk, indicate that the monitoring sys­
tem is not working well in high sentiment periods,
raising the need for regulators to take various mea­
sures to strengthen their monitoring mechanisms
to reduce the possible negative impact of high
investor sentiment.
Second, we use a firm-level investor sentiment
variable, unlike earlier studies that adopt a timelevel measure. It is appropriate for Korean stock

adding empirical results.
Third, we provide a new factor affecting initial
engagement discounts. Prior research has found
the cause of fee discounting in audit-related char­

partners (Huang et al. 2015). This study shows that
high sentiment, a non-audit characteristic, can also
affect audit risk assessment, thereby lowering initial
audit fees. Our results suggest that initial audit fees
should be investigated from various perspectives
that affect managerial or auditor decision-making.
The remainder of this study is organized as
follows. Section 2 reviews prior studies and devel­

respectively. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

II. Literature review and hypothesis
development
Prior research on initial audit fee discount

Initial discounting is an important issue for practi­
tioners and academics in the auditing field because
it can impair auditors’ independence or audit qual­
ity for continuous future contracts by lowering
audit hours in response to decreased audit fees
(Cohen Commission 1978; DeAngelo 1981; Dye
1991; Eshleman and Guo 2014). Initial auditors
may discount their audit fees to satisfy new clients
expecting lower audit fees or to win in price com­
petition (Beattie and Fearnley 1995). DeAngelo
(1981) argues that it is a sunk cost that does not
affect the independence of auditors because it is ‘a

962

Y. YUN AND H. A. KIM

competitive response to the expectation of future
quasi-rents to incumbent auditors’. However,
Simon and Francis (1988) mention that initial dis­
counting plays a role in impairing audit quality
based on prospect theory suggesting that people
give more value to gains than losses. Consistent
with this, Public Company Accounting Oversight
Board (PCAOB) (2011, 17) states, ‘If auditors bid
on new engagements with the assumption that they
will lose money in the first years of an engagement
but recoup that loss over a long period of time, the
problem may be unrealistic pricing, with
a resulting effect on audit effort or resources at
the beginning of an auditor-client relationship’.
Turner (2002) and Hansen (2010) show concerns
over the possibility that low audit fees may lead to
deterioration in audit quality.
Empirical evidence is mixed and differs
depending on the country or time. Specifically,
studies examining the US audit market report
the existence of an initial discounting beha­
viour. For example, Simon and Francis (1988)
find that initial audit fees are lower by about
24% in the first year and 15% in the next two
years, and Ettredge and Greenberg (1990)
report a 24% discount. Ghosh and Lustgarten
(2006) are motivated by the different competi­
tions in small and large audit firms and show
that fee discounting is more prevalent in nonBig4 than Big4. Recent studies have analysed
the existence of initial discounting in the preand post-SOX periods, but the results are still
mixed. Huang, Raghunandan, and Rama (2009)
report the initial audit fee premium for Big4
auditors in the post-SOX period, but Desir,
Casterella, and Kokina (2014) find that initial
fee discounting exists in both Big4 and nonBig4 from 2007 to 2010, in the post-SOX per­
iod. In the UK audit market, prior studies pro­
vide relatively consistent evidence on the
existence of initial discounting (Gregory and
Collier 1996; Peel 2013), but the empirical
results of the Australian audit market are
mixed (Francis 1984; Craswell and Francis
1999). Regarding the Korean audit market,
prior studies provide evidence that auditors
discount their initial fees (Rho, Bae, and Cho
2004; Lee, Park, and Jeong 2011; Jeong and Ki
2016). In addition, Shin, Choi, and Cho (2007)

find a negative association between initial dis­
counts and initial audit quality, supporting the
concern raised by regulators or academics.

Prior research on investor sentiment and financial
reporting

The literature has analysed the effect of investor senti­
ment on corporate decisions (e.g. Grundy and Li
2010), but the accounting field has just started to
examine its influence on managers and analysts. In
terms of managers, Hurwitz (2018) reports that high
investor sentiment is associated with optimistic man­
agement forecasts and interprets the result as evidence
of managerial vulnerability to investor sentiment.
Consistent with this, Brown et al. (2012) find that
pro forma earnings disclosures tend to exclude recur­
ring and non-recurring expenses in high investor
sentiment. Bergman and Roychowdhury (2008)
show that managers are less likely to disclose longhorizon earnings forecasts in high investor sentiment
because they want to maintain investor optimism.
Furthermore, prior studies examining the effect
of investor sentiment on analysts’ behaviour pro­
vide evidence that analysts are also susceptible to
high investor sentiment. Specifically, Hribar and
McInnis (2012) find that analysts forecast the longterm earnings of firms with high uncertainty more
optimistically in high investor sentiment periods.
Walther and Willis (2013) use the short-run quar­
terly earnings forecast of sell-side analysts and find
that they tend to forecast more optimistically and
less accurately when investor sentiment is high.
Several studies investigate the relationship between
investor sentiment and financial reporting such as
accruals or conservatism. Ali and Gurun (2009)
examine the association between investor sentiment
and accruals anomaly and find greater accruals for
small firms in high sentiment periods, supporting
investors’ limited attention to accruals and managers’
opportunistic usage. Simpson (2013) extends this
study by adding evidence that managers use accruals
to meet or beat analysts’ forecasts during high inves­
tor sentiment periods. Contrary to previous results,
Ge, Seybert, and Zhang (2019) show that managers
are likely to report conservatively in high sentiment
times to reduce potential litigation risk due to stock
price decline.

Hypothesis development

Extending the literature to auditors’ risk assess­
ment, Amin, Eshleman, and Guo (2021a) investi­
gate external auditors’ reactions to investor
sentiment using the US data and find that audit
fees are lower in high sentiment periods despite the
high likelihood of misstatements. They additionally
show that market reactions to misstatements are
less negative in high sentiment periods and argue
that a decrease in auditors’ legal risk leads to lower
audit fees.
Audit fees consist of audit effort costs and
expected liability losses arising from audited finan­
cial statements (Simunic 1980; Seetharaman, Gul,
and Lynn 2002). The positive association between
litigation risk and audit fees in prior studies indi­
cates that it plays an important role in determining
audit fees (Seetharaman, Gul, and Lynn 2002; Choi
et al. 2009). Countries have different legal liabilities
depending on their legal regimes, which vary the
degree of litigation liability reflected in audit fees
(Choi et al. 2009). Therefore, if the prior finding
based on the US which has a strong legal environ­
ment is mainly related to litigation risk, the result is
unlikely to be found in countries with weak legal
regimes.
However, engagement risk includes reputation
and regulation risks as well as litigation risk
(DeFond and Zhang 2014). Auditors in less liti­
gious environments do not expect a significant
reduction in litigation risk from high sentiment,
but investors’ positive biases can entice auditors
to conduct less conservative audits, expecting
investors to be tolerant of financial statement
errors. This may decrease audit fees by lowering
auditors’ reputation risk.
In addition, auditors are affected by mood states
(Chung, Cohen, and Monroe 2008; Cianci and
Bierstaker 2009). Chung, Cohen, and Monroe
(2008) use the experiment with Australian auditing
students and find that a positive mood is associated
with less conservative inventory valuation. Cianci
and Bierstaker (2009) show that auditors in the
experiment are good at hypothesis generation
tasks, such as cause analysis of fluctuations under
a negative mood. Even if investors’ high sentiment
may not influence auditors’ mood directly, there is
a possibility that the favourable atmosphere

963

surrounding a firm in the stock market can affect
auditors’ engagement risk evaluation, thereby
reducing audit fees.
To examine the effect of high sentiment on
auditor risk assessment in Korea with relatively
low litigation risk, we focus on auditor change.
With all other things equal, it increases audit
engagement risk because of high information risk
derived from the lack of knowledge about a newly
accepted auditee (Francis et al. 2017). If the influ­
ence of high sentiment is not limited to litigation
risk, audit fees are expected to be lower when firms
change auditors. However, auditors require higher
audit fees in response to future misstatements of
firms that engage in audit opinion shopping during
high sentiment times (Amin, Eshleman, and Guo
2021b). Therefore, it is difficult to predict the effect
of high sentiment on initial audit fees in a weak
legal environment. Thus, we set our hypothesis as
follows.
Hypothesis. High investor sentiment has no influ­
ence on initial audit fees in a less litigious country.

III. Research design
Individual firm-level investor sentiment measure

In this study, we use firm-level investor sentiment
to examine its effect on initial audit fees.
Specifically, we measure the variable based on
Ryu, Kim, and Yang (2017) to fit the Korean
stock market. Baker and Wurgler (2006) suggest
a market-based index of sentiment using six
proxies: the closed-end fund discount rate, NYSE
stock turnover, the number of IPOs, average firstday returns on IPOs, the share of equity issues, and
the dividend premium. However, it is difficult to
apply this method to Korean studies. This is
because: (1) unlike the US stock market, the
Korean stock market has a small margin of rate
depending on dividends; thus, we cannot use the
dividend premium; (2) the number of IPOs in the
Korean stock market is significantly less than that
in the US, so it is not appropriate to use IPOrelated variables; (3) there are insufficient data on
the discount rate for closed-end funds. Therefore,
prior Korean studies claim that it is appropriate to

1984; Gul and Goodwin 2010). We also include
auditor-related variables, which are indicator
variables for Big 4 auditors (BIG4), industry spe­
cialist auditors (SPEC), and non-qualified audit
opinion (OPIN) (Bae, Choi, and Lee 2018).
Additionally, the model includes indicator vari­
ables for KOSDAQ-listed firms (KOSDAQ) and
designated firms (DSG) to capture the character­
istics of Korean market. Finally, we include year
fixed effects and industry fixed effects based on
two-digit Korean SIC. Note that all of our ana­
lysed results are based on individual firm level
clustered standard errors (Peterson 2009), and all
continuous variables are winsorized at 1% for
both tails.

Definition of variables is as follows. LNAF is the natural logarithm number of total audit fees; LNAH is the natural logarithm number of total audit hours;
SENTIMENT is individual firm-level investor sentiment measure calculated based on the first principle component on five proxies; CHANGE a dummy variable
that takes 1 if auditor of the firm is changed for the period, otherwise 0; SIZE is the natural logarithm number of total assets; ROA is operating income scaled
by total asset; LEV is total liabilities divided by total assets; BM is book value of equity divided by market value of the firm; INVREC is sum of inventory and
account receivable scaled by total asset; QUICK is current liability divided by current asset; NEWFIN is a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm increase the
book value of equity during period, otherwise 0; LOSS is a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm reported a net loss, otherwise 0; BIG4 is a dummy variable
that takes 1 if the firm is audited by Big 4 auditors (PwC, KPMG, Deloitte, and EY), otherwise 0; SPEC is a measure of industry specialist auditors defined as
a dummy variable takes 1 if auditor has a portion over 30% within the industry and has a gap with a following auditor over 10%, based on client total sales.
The market share is calculated as the sum of the auditor’s client total sales for each industry-year divided by the sum of all auditors’ client total sales in the
industry-year; OPIN is a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm receive audit opinion except for qualified opinion, otherwise 0; ACCLAG is lagged total accruals
divided by lagged total asset, accruals is calculated by subtracting operating cash flow from net income; KOSDAQ is a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm is
listed in KOSDAQ market, otherwise 0; DSG is a control for designation in Korean audit market, a dummy variable that takes 1 if total audit fee is more than
doubled over previous period, otherwise 0.

This study has the following limitations. First,
we measure the investor sentiment variable fol­
lowing prior studies (e.g. Ryu, Kim, and Yang
2017), but it is a modified version of Baker and
Wurgler (2006) which is appropriate for the
Korean environment. Therefore, our findings
may not be comparable to other studies measur­
ing the variable based on Baker and Wurgler
(2006).
Second, individual investors account for twothirds of the Korean stock market and have spec­
ulative and behaviourally biased characteristics
(e.g. Chung, Park, and Ryu 2016). Therefore, our
results may not be generalizable to other countries
whose individual investors and stock market are
characterized differently.

Variables
Definition
LNAF
the natural logarithm number of total audit fees
LNAH
the natural logarithm number of total audit hours
SENTIMENT individual firm-level investor sentiment measure calculated
based on the first principle component on five proxies
CHANGE
a dummy variable that takes 1 if auditor of the firm is
changed for the period, otherwise 0
SIZE
the natural logarithm number of total assets
ROA
operating income scaled by total asset
LEV
total liabilities divided by total assets
BM
book value of equity divided by market value of the firm
INVREC
sum of inventory and account receivable scaled by total
asset
QUICK
current liability divided by current asset
NEWFIN
a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm increase the book
value of equity during period, otherwise 0
LOSS
a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm reported a net loss,
otherwise 0
BIG4
a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm is audited by Big 4
auditors (PwC, KPMG, Deloitte, and EY), otherwise 0
SPEC
a measure of industry specialist auditors defined as a dummy
variable takes 1 if auditor has a portion over 30% within
the industry and has a gap with a following auditor over
10%, based on client total sales. The market share is
calculated as the sum of the auditor’s client total sales for
each industry-year divided by the sum of all auditors’
client total sales in the industry-year.
OPIN
a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm receive audit
opinion except for qualified opinion, otherwise 0
ACCLAG
lagged total accruals divided by lagged total asset, accruals is
calculated by subtracting operating cash flow from net
income
KOSDAQ
a dummy variable that takes 1 if the firm is listed in KOSDAQ
market, otherwise 0
DSG
a control for designation in Korean audit market, a dummy
variable that takes 1 if total audit fee is more than doubled
over previous period, otherwise 0
