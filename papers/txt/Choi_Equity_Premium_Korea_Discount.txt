This study examines the cost of equity capital (COC) and the factors that inﬂuence
the COC of listed Korean companies, and compares the COC of Korean companies
with that of companies from 31 selected countries. The major research question is
whether companies that are listed in an emerging market have a disadvantage as they
are underpriced through a higher COC (as compared with companies that are traded
in developed markets). Consistent with the “Korea Discount,” we ﬁnd that the COC
is signiﬁcantly higher for Korean companies than for companies in other countries
after controlling for other relevant factors. However, the “Korea Discount” has signiﬁcantly eased in recent years.
Keywords: analysts’ forecasts; cost of capital; ﬁrm characteristics; Korea Discount;
Korean stock market
JEL Codes: G00, G18, M20

Introduction
The purpose of this study is to examine the expected rates of return of companies that
are listed in the Korean stock market by investigating: (1) the implied cost of capital
(COC); (2) equity premia; and (3) factors that affect the expected rate of return.1
A plethora of anecdotal evidence has shown the existence of the so-called “Korea
Discount.” This discount refers to the valuation phenomenon whereby, in comparison
with listed companies in other countries, listed Korean companies are, ceteris paribus,
traded at a discount. Figure 1 illustrates that the price-to-earnings (P/E) and price-tobook (P/B) ratios for Korean companies are signiﬁcantly lower than those for the rest
of the sample, suggesting that listed Korean companies are substantially undervalued
even when compared with companies from other Asian developing countries (Salmon
2007, Suh and Sim 2007). We observe the “Korea Discount” even though Korean companies exhibit greater proﬁtability than companies in other countries. Concerning the
“Korea Discount,” Guerrera (2006) commented:
HSBC analysts estimate that over the past 12 years, the Korean stock market had
traded, on average, 26 per cent below the valuation of other exchanges in Asia excluding
Japan.

In the late 1990s, Korea experienced serious economic difﬁculties. The collapse of the
Thai baht in 1997 triggered a regional economic crisis that spread to other Asian
countries, including Korea. Korean stock prices consequently plummeted, devastating
the Korean market for several years. Since the economic turmoil, the attention Korea
has been paying to corporate transparency has signiﬁcantly increased and the Korean
government has expressed great concern about the “Korea Discount.” Korean authorities
have consequently taken a series of steps to restructure the Korean ﬁnancial system
(e.g. improving corporate governance and transparency) and, in early 2009, the Korean
government even launched the national brand committee to mitigate the widespread
“Korea Discount.”
In an effort to tackle the “Korea Discount” and increase market transparency, the
Korean government decided to adopt the International Financial Reporting Standards
(IFRS): all listed companies with assets greater than $2 billion are required to adopt
IFRS starting from 2011. Several companies early-adopted IFRS in 2009 (e.g. STX and
Pulmuone holdings). Market participants, including analysts, investors, and ﬁnancial
authorities, expect that the adoption of IFRS will mitigate the market stigma of the
“Korea Discount” by reducing uncertainty and the incidence of opaque accounting practices (Ramstad 2007a, Moon 2008).
The attention of Korean authorities has also been recently directed to promoting
South Korea from the category of “emerging” markets to “developed status” in terms of
the FTSE stock market index (the upgrade transpired in September 2009).2 The government and market participants have expressed the hope that the status upgrade can abate
the “Korea Discount” through the increased trust of investors in the Korean ﬁnancial
market. The status change also highlights the importance of the timing of this study as
our ﬁndings can be helpful in determining the changes that accompany the status
upgrade and early adoption of IFRS.
An investigation of the phenomenon of the “Korea Discount” would ponder several
fundamental questions. Is the Korean stock market really undervalued after taking into

account differences in growth prospects? How do we measure the valuation difference?
In our study, we seek to provide answers to the above questions in two steps. We ﬁrst
estimate the implied cost of capital for companies in the Korean stock market; then, we
compare it with the COC for companies in other countries. Despite the contribution of
previous literature, the link between the equity cost of capital and its effects on stock
market undervaluation, such as the “Korea Discount,” is far from obvious. Having a
direct effect on the present value of future economic beneﬁts, the COC is a crucial factor in company valuation. Assuming the same long-term growth rate for all companies,
on average, higher (respectively lower) ﬁrm value stems from a lower (respectively
higher) COC. Hence, the valuation level of the stock market may be examined by estimating the implied costs of capital that are implicitly required by market participants
after controlling for the expected growth prospects.
Another major research question is whether, when compared with companies in
developed markets, companies that are listed in an emerging market (e.g. the Korean
stock market) have a disadvantage as they are underpriced through higher implied costs
of capital. We compare the COC of Korean companies with that of US companies and
examine the exact nature of the relationship between the COC and the undervaluation
of Korean corporations. The estimation of the COC in Korea and its comparison with
the COC in the USA helps us to ascertain whether the COC in Korea can be regarded
as a factor of the “Korea Discount.” In addition, we compare the risk premia of the two
countries, since the equity risk premium is directly related to market valuation (Foerster
and Karolyi 1999). Prior literature suggests that when they successfully reduce the
aforementioned shortcomings of their ﬁnancial system, emerging markets experience an
upward revaluation of their shares (Miller 1999, Henry 2000), which is achieved mainly
through the decrease of the COC (Henry 2000). We also investigate whether the undervaluation of the Korean stock market has eased up in recent years.
Finally, we estimate the COC for 32 countries and investigate its association with
valuation measures after controlling for macro-economic variables such as the Gross
Domestic Product (GDP), inﬂation, level of corruption and governance, and ﬁnancial
reporting quality.
Our results suggest that although a substantial country risk premium still exists in
the Korean stock market, the implied costs of capital have decreased over recent years.
These ﬁndings are in line with the argument that the “Korea Discount” has eased. Furthermore, we ﬁnd a signiﬁcant association between the measure of the implied cost of
capital and variables that affect the risk and proﬁtability, as perceived by investors. The
implication of our ﬁndings may be extended to other countries.
Literature review
Extant literature dealing with the “Korea Discount” and its various aspects has pointed
out several issues pertaining to the study of this phenomenon. One is the difﬁculty of
measuring the extent of the “Korea Discount.” Numerous business articles have
attempted to measure the level of the “Korea Discount” and speculated about its causes,
but most of these studies have examined the levels of ﬁnancial ratios, such as the P/E
or P/B benchmarks. However, the validity of such studies depends on appropriate measures of valuation. Even though surveys of managers afﬁrm the importance of the P/E
and/or P/B ratios in valuing stocks (Shahed et al. 2008), the nature of the connection
and the factors linking these ratios to stock-market valuation is less obvious (Kumar
and Hyodo 2001, Bhargava and Malhotra 2006). The use of ﬁnancial ratios in measur-

ing the level of the stock-market discount may lead to spurious inferences since their
level may reﬂect not only the amount of risk but also other underlying economic factors
of the concerned country (e.g. growth prospects, business sectors, interest rates, and
size, etc.; Byrne and Saibeni 2000, Park 2000, Kumar and Hyodo 2001). Thus, for
example, lower P/E or P/B ratios may indicate lower implied future growth prospects
of companies in the country.3
The second issue is the choice of methodology in measuring the cost of capital. In
many cases, academics and practitioners rely on the COC as estimated by methods based
on historical data, such as the capital asset pricing model (CAPM). However, CAPM
estimates have been recently criticized as having limited practical use (Fama and French
1997, 2002). To mitigate this problem, we estimate the expected rates of return by
reverse-engineering the residual earnings model, which has been used by a large number
of studies to test hypotheses related to the link between the required rates of return and
relevant factors that may affect them (Dhaliwal et al. 2005, Daske 2006, Easton and
Sommers 2007). In this study, we use Easton et al.’s (2002; henceforth ETSS) model to
simultaneously estimate the growth rate and the COC; we also employ the Ohlson and
Juettner-Nauroth (2005) model to estimate the ﬁrm-speciﬁc COC for 32 countries.
Only in the past decade have reverse-engineered valuation models been used to
determine the COC; nevertheless, the number of studies that use the COC that is estimated from the reverse-engineered residual earnings or abnormal earnings growth
model has recently been increasing (O’Hanlon and Steele 2000, Claus and Thomas
2001, Gebhardt et al. 2001, Easton et al. 2002, Baginski and Wahlen 2003, Gode and
Mohanram 2003, Easton 2004, Easton and Monahan 2005, Easton and Sommers 2007).
Having been developed to estimate the required rates of return at various levels of
aggregation, reverse-engineered valuation methods can be used to make ﬁrm-speciﬁc
estimates (Claus and Thomas 2001; Gebhardt et al. 2001, Baginski and Wahlen 2003,
Gode and Mohanram 2003, Ohlson and Juettner-Nauroth 2005) as well as estimates for
a group or portfolio (O’Hanlon and Steele 2000, Claus and Thomas 2001, Easton et al.
2002, Easton 2004). However, ﬁrm-speciﬁc estimation requires assumptions about the
long-term growth rate (e.g. fading ROE to the industry median ROE (Gebhardt et al.
2001) or the same rate for the long-term growth of abnormal earnings for all companies
(Claus and Thomas 2001, Gode and Mohanram 20034), which can be regarded as a disadvantage of this approach.
The literature has also documented international differences in the COC and investigated the link between the required rate of return and potential factors (Agmon and
Findlay 1982, Damodaran 2003, Chen et al. 2004, Koedijk and van Dijk 2004, Sabal
2004, Dargenidou et al. 2006, Daske 2006, Hail and Leuz 2006, He and Kryzanowski
2007). Several explanations have also been proposed regarding the questions of whether
and why companies that are listed on the Korean stock market are considered to be
undervalued,5 and a close link has been reported between undervaluation and corruption
or political risk (i.e. North Korea) (Lee and Ng 2006, Pyo and Jung 2007, Pantzalis
et al. 2008).

Model estimation
Anecdotal evidence shows that the Korean stock market is undervalued; consequently,
ﬁrms that are listed in the Korean stock exchange have to bear a signiﬁcantly higher
COC than their economic fundamentals warrant. In this section, we examine whether,
after we control for macro-economic factors, the Korea Discount is due to a high COC.
Our empirical model includes those macro-economic variables that prior studies have
found relevant to country valuation: inﬂation,9 GDP, Corruption Perception Index (CPI),
country governance, and ﬁnancial reporting quality. We expect the inﬂation variable to
be positively correlated with the COC and negatively correlated with ﬁrm value. The
GDP variable is represented by the natural logarithm of the GDP of the country
expressed in millions of USD (Easterly 1993, Lee and Ng 2006).10 From a theoretical
standpoint, GDP has a positive (negative) association with ﬁrm value (cost of capital).
Extant literature has reported that companies listed in the stock markets of corrupted
countries are undervalued (Lee and Ng 2006, Pantzalis et al. 2008), suggesting that the
corruption level is a critical factor for country valuation. As a proxy for the level of corruption, we use the CPI provided by Transparency International.11 The CPI index is a
comprehensive measure of corruption based on data collected from up to 13 sources

including the IMD, World Bank, Political and Economic Risk Consultancy, and World
Economic Forum, ranging from 1 (extremely corrupted) to 10 (extremely clean).
Prior studies report that ﬁrms from countries with stronger regulations and stricter
law enforcement systems have lower COCs (Hail and Leuz 2006). We utilize the
Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) issued by the World Bank, which covers 212
countries (Kaoufmann et al. 2008). The WGI comprises six different categories of measures that reﬂect the extent to which a country’s governance system is functioning well:
(1) voice and accountability (VA); (2) political stability and absence of violence (PV);
(3) government effectiveness (GE); (4) regulatory quality (RQ); (5) rule of law (RL);
and (6) control of corruption (CC).
According to the World Bank’s webpage, the ﬁrst dimension, VA, measures the
“perceptions of the extent to which a country’s citizens are able to participate in selecting their government, as well as freedom of expression, freedom of association, and a
free media.” The second dimension, PV, measures the “perceptions of the likelihood
that the government will be destabilized or overthrown by unconstitutional or violent
means, including politically-motivated violence and terrorism,” and the third dimension,
GE, deals with the “perceptions of the quality of public services, the quality of the civil
service and the degree of its independence from political pressures, the quality of policy
formulation and implementation, and the credibility of the government’s commitment to
such policies.” RQ is deﬁned as referring to the degree of “the ability of the government to formulate and implement sound policies and regulations that permit and promote private sector development,” and RL as “the extent to which agents have
conﬁdence in and abide by the rules of society, and in particular the quality of contract
enforcement, property rights, the police, and the courts, as well as the likelihood of
crime and violence.” Finally, the last dimension, CC, reﬂects the “extent to which public power is exercised for private gain, including both petty and grand forms of corruption, as well as ‘capture’ of the state by elites and private interests.”12
Many studies have shown that ﬁnancial reporting quality varies across countries and
regions. Thus, we can assume that these differences may potentially inﬂuence the COC
estimates through earnings forecasts and accounting measures and that lower reporting
quality is likely to be accompanied by a signiﬁcantly higher COC. To control for the
effect of differences in ﬁnancial reporting quality, our analysis includes control variables
that represent proxies for ﬁnancial quality. In line with the literature, we use three different categories of measures, viz. faithful representation of operating performance
(FROP), Accounting Conservatism (AC), and quality of accruals (QA).
FROP, as measured by the discretionary accrual model below, reﬂects the extent of
earnings management (i.e. management discretion over accruals). We assume that companies that display lower ﬁnancial reporting quality will tend to engage in earnings
management more extensively. In our study, the measures of FROP are the negatives of
the median of absolute value of the estimated discretionary accruals.

Data and sample selection
The ﬁnancial data used in the study were collected from companies listed in the Korean
stock market. We collected annual ﬁnancial data for the period 2000 through 2007.14
Companies with a non-December ﬁscal year-end were excluded from the sample. The
Korean sample consisted of companies that traded on either the Korea Stock exchange
or the Korea Securities Dealers Automated Quotation (KOSDAQ) exchange.15
Measures for the ﬁnancial variables were taken from ﬁnancial statements and the
stock market at the end of the ﬁscal year. All per share variables for Korean companies
were adjusted for stock splits and stock dividends. Accounting data, including earnings
per share, book value, sales, long-term debt, total assets, and number of shares were
culled from the DataGuide Pro annual research ﬁles.16 Analysts’ forecasts, stock prices,
market betas, and risk-free rates (T-bond rates) were also obtained from DataGuide Pro
database.17 Earnings forecasts were derived from the last consensus forecasts available
in December.
The sample selection rule requires earnings forecasts for two years. For example, to
be included in the sample of 2007, the last consensus forecasts of the sample ﬁrms
available in December 2007 should include ﬁscal years ending on 31 December 2007
and 2008. The top and bottom percentiles of observations based on the COC, P/E, and
P/B were simultaneously eliminated to mitigate the effect of extreme values. For the
international comparison, accounting numbers and analysts’ forecasts are culled from
the I/B/E/S database, and risk-free rates (T-bond rates), GDP, and inﬂation rates are
obtained from the Datastream database. The ﬁnancial reporting quality is computed
from the North American and global Compustat database. Finally, to investigate the
impact of the country’s corruption on the ﬁrm value and the COC, we use the CPI for
the years 2000 through 2007 provided by Transparency International.18 The WGI index
is from the World Bank (Kaoufmann et al. 2008). To be included in the international
sample, each country is required to have at least 20 observations. Covering the years
2000 through 2007, the ﬁnal sample consists of 8214 observations across 32 countries.

The numerator of the P/B and P/E ratios, viz. market price, is based on the expected
future earnings that market participants pay for (Ohlson 1995). If participants expect
higher future economic beneﬁts relative to the book value (earnings), the P/B (P/E) will
show a higher value as the market’s expectations are incorporated in the numerator.
Prior literature has reported that low P/B ﬁrms face higher systematic risk and earn
higher ex post returns than high P/B ﬁrms (Fama and French 1992, Berk et al. 1999).

of companies. COC for the highest quintile of Tobin’s Q (17.7%) is signiﬁcantly lower
than that for the lowest quintile (23.6%).

It is also well documented that the COC is positively correlated with ﬁrm risk. The
measures we use to assess ﬁrm risk in this study are the market beta (Beta), debt-tomarket (D/M), and debt-to-assets (D/A). In addition, we also use the CAPM beta to
capture the ﬁrm-speciﬁc risk that is related to market volatility (Sharpe 1964, Lintner
1965, Mossin 1966). Given that, a positive association between Beta and the COC
would be expected (Gordon and Gordon 1997, Gode and Mohanram 2003, Harris et al.
2003). Contrary to these expectations, although the pattern is less apparent, Beta and
the COC are associated negatively. The value of r varies between 21.1% (portfolio 1)
and 19.0% (portfolio 5), but stands at 18.7% for portfolio 3. The result is not surprising
in the sense that the COCs that are estimated from the historic beta (i.e. the CAPM) are
of limited use in predicting ﬁrm-speciﬁc risk.21
D/M and D/A measure the risk that is associated with the ﬁnancial leverage of the
ﬁrm. As the amount of debt in a ﬁrm’s capital structure increases, so too does the risk
that the ﬁrm takes on. This provides an incentive for market participants to require
higher rates of returns for ﬁrms with higher ratios. We expect a positive association
between the COC and ﬁnancial leverage. It turns out that r for the lowest quintile of
D/A is 18.4%; for the highest quintile, r is 22.3%.
ROA and ROE proxy the proﬁtability of companies. According to our results, they
are negatively associated with the COC. While r for the lowest quintiles of ROA and
ROE is 27.1 and 26.7%, respectively, for the highest quintiles, it is 18.2 and 20.4%,
respectively.

Concluding remarks
A variety of evidence has shown the existence of the “Korea Discount,” referring to the
phenomenon that the companies listed in the Korean stock market are, ceteris paribus,
traded at a discount in comparison with companies in other countries. The market
stigma of the “Korea Discount” has spread widely to other economic areas including
the national brand value and foreign direct investment (FDI).25
The results of this research have several important implications for the study of
equity premia. First, this paper provides compelling evidence that the Korean stock
market is traded at a discount. The estimated equity premium over the risk-free rate for

Asia-Paciﬁc Journal of Accounting & Economics

161

the Korean stock market averages 15.1% over the years 2000 through 2007, which is
signiﬁcantly higher than that reported for the US stock market. Second, our study
shows that the equity premium has decreased over the last eight years. We postulate
that the economic turmoil of the 1997 ﬁnancial crisis negatively (in a valuation sense)
affected the Korean stock market in several subsequent years and investors’ required
rates were much higher after the crisis as many companies had ﬁnancial difﬁculties if
not already in default.
However, the “Korea Discount” has eased up in recent years. It appears that the
market disadvantage has decreased since the late 1990s, partly due to the efforts of the
government to enhance corporate transparency including early adoption of the IFRS or
advancement to “developed status” according to the FTSE stock market index. It is also
expected that the status promotion to “advanced” country based on the MSCI index,
which is supposed to be decided in June 2010, will also provide a positive signal to
potential investors regarding the level of development and safety of the Korean ﬁnancial
market.
As well documented in prior studies, we found a signiﬁcant association between
ﬁrm characteristics and the COC (Gebhardt et al. 2001, Gode and Mohanram 2003,
Botosan and Plumlee 2005). To investigate this issue, we examined a measure of the
implied cost of capital in relation to variables that affect the risk and proﬁtability, as
perceived by market investors.
Finally, after controlling for macro variables such as GDP, inﬂation, a country’s
level of corruption, the WGI, and ﬁnancial reporting quality, we estimated the COC for
32 countries and found that the Korean stock market is substantially undervalued.
Nevertheless, the results of this study should be interpreted with some caveats. One
issue to be wary of is the potential effect of omitted ﬁrm-level variables on the estimated COC. For example, ﬁrm-level disclosure and/or information quality may have a
signiﬁcant impact on the implied cost of capital (Diamond and Verrecchia 1991, Botosan and Plumlee 2002, Easley and O’Hara 2004, Berger et al. 2006, Core et al. 2007,
Lambert et al. 2007).
Another issue is the potential upward bias in the estimates of the costs of capital
implied by market prices and analysts’ forecasts. The high costs of capital may reﬂect
the fact that earnings forecasts are optimistically biased. If so, the COC that is implied
by inﬂated analysts’ forecasts of earnings will be upwardly biased as well (Easton and
Sommers 2007). A related problem is that earnings forecasts that are prepared under
different accounting regimes (e.g. GAAP or Korean GAAP) may lead to differences in
the COC (Easton 2006); different GAAP may thus affect estimates of r.
Future research can fruitfully extend this study in several directions. One valuable
area for future study would be an analysis of analysts’ optimism and the COC using a
dataset that spans a longer time-window. Besides, since caution must be exercised when
we interpret omitted ﬁrm-level variables, further research is required to investigate the
link between disclosure or information quality and the phenomenon of stock market discounts. Another worthwhile area of research would be one that looks into cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses of analysts’ forecast errors for Korean companies and
investigates the extent to which forecast errors are attributable to the COC. Further
investigation of cross-cultural differences among countries would also lead to fruitful
results.
Overall, this study is among the ﬁrst to examine the implied equity premium for the
Korean stock market. Our research on the links between the implied cost of capital and

interpretation of the “Korea Discount” based on the equity premium.
