ABSTRACT

KEYWORDS

As ESG issues are increasingly viewed as financially material, ESG shareholder engagement has
become an important channel through which institutional investors seek to influence corporate
behaviour and enhance firm value. This study investigates whether ESG engagement by influential
institutional investors affects firm value and how corporate governance moderates this relation­
ship. Using 3104 firm-year observations of Korean listed firms from 2020 to 2023, we conduct fixedeffects panel regressions to analyse the valuation effects of ESG shareholder engagement. We find
consistent evidence that ESG engagement by influential institutional investors enhances firm
value. Specifically, disclosures by the National Pension Service (NPS) – the largest and most
influential institutional investor in Korea – of its intention to engage firms on ESG issues are
associated with a significant increase in firm value in the following year. Furthermore, this positive
association is significantly moderated by corporate governance quality, that is, stronger for firms
with weaker internal governance structures. These results indicate that ESG shareholder engage­
ment operates as a substitute external governance mechanism, mitigating deficiencies in internal
governance and contributing to firm value creation. Overall, the study highlights the value
relevance of investor-driven ESG engagement and underscores the importance of governance
context in shaping its effectiveness.

I. Introduction

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG)
considerations have become a central component
of corporate strategy and investment decisionmaking, rather than a niche or purely ethical con­
cern. A rapidly expanding body of empirical
research demonstrates that ESG issues are finan­
cially material and increasingly incorporated into
investment, monitoring, and stewardship decisions
by institutional investors (Atz et al. 2023; Dimson,
Karakas, and Li 2015; Friede, Busch, and Bassen
2015). As of March 2024, 4827 institutions had
signed the Principles for Responsible Investment
(PRI), representing US$128.4 trillion in assets
under management, underscoring the growing
role of ESG in global capital markets. Consistent
with this trend, McCahery, Sautner, and Starks
(2016) report that 63% of institutional investors
have directly engaged with corporate management
on strategic and policy issues, including ESG, over
the preceding five years.

A large and rapidly expanding literature has
examined the relationship between ESG perfor­
mance and firm value. Early studies often viewed
ESG activities as a cost that could undermine
shareholder value by diverting resources away
from profit maximization (Friedman 2007; Vance
1975). More recent research, however, increasingly
characterizes ESG as a strategic investment that
enhances long-term competitiveness, improves
access to capital, and strengthens corporate reputa­
tion (Fatemi and Fooladi 2013; Fatemi, Glaum, and
Kaiser 2018). Empirical evidence suggests that ESG
performance affects firm value through multiple
channels, including reduced information asymme­
try, lower cost of capital, enhanced investor trust,
and improved risk management (Fatemi, Fooladi,
and Tehranian 2015).
Event-based studies further highlight the riskmitigating role of ESG. Using ESG-related news
events, Krüger (2015) shows that while positive
ESG events generate limited market reactions,

tance of ESG performance, far less is known about
the role of investors in actively shaping firms’ ESG
practices and the valuation effects of such engage­
ment. In contrast to the extensive literature on ESG
outcomes, relatively few studies examine ESG
shareholder engagement itself as a distinct govern­
ance mechanism. A growing strand of research
emphasizes that ESG shareholder engagement is
not random but depends on several economic and
institutional factors, including the financial materi­
ality of ESG issues, investors’ ownership stakes and
influence, engagement costs, and investment hor­
izons (Barko, Cremers, and Renneboog 2022;
Dimson, Karakas, and Li 2015). In particular, long-

term institutional investors such as pension funds
have strong incentives to engage with firms on ESG
issues to mitigate downside risks and protect longterm portfolio value (Brav et al. 2008; Hoepner
et al. 2024; Semenova and Hassel 2019; Starks,
Venkat, and Zhu 2017).
Importantly, several seminal and recent studies
provide direct evidence that investors’ ESG engage­
ment can influence firm value. Dimson, Karakas,
and Li (2015) show that successful ESG engage­
ments are associated with positive abnormal
returns and improvements in operating perfor­
mance. Barko, Cremers, and Renneboog (2022)
document that firms targeted by ESG engagement
experience significant improvements in ESG per­
formance and generate excess returns relative to
non-target firms. More recently, Bauer, Derwall,
and Tissen (2023) and Hoepner et al. (2024)
demonstrate that ESG engagement on financially
material issues enhances profitability, reduces
downside risk, and contributes to long-term firm
value. These studies suggest that ESG shareholder
engagement can create value through economically
meaningful channels rather than merely reflecting
ethical preferences.
Despite these advances, existing studies largely
focus on engagement outcomes in developed
Western markets and primarily examine average
effects, paying limited attention to how internal
corporate governance conditions shape the effec­
tiveness of ESG shareholder engagement. As
a result, it remains unclear whether ESG engage­
ment complements strong internal governance
structures or instead functions as a substitute
mechanism when internal governance is weak.
Moreover, empirical evidence from markets char­
acterized by a dominant institutional investor
remains scarce, limiting our understanding of
how investor influence and governance context
jointly determine the value effects of ESG
engagement.
Korea provides a unique institutional setting to
address these unresolved issues. The Korean
Stewardship Code, enacted in December 2016,
was designed to promote active ownership by insti­
tutional investors. In 2018, the National Pension
Service (NPS)-one of the world’s largest public
pension funds – adopted the Stewardship Code
and subsequently expanded its ESG shareholder

engagement activities. Although most NPS engage­
ments are conducted privately, a regulatory reform
to the Financial Investment Services and Capital
Markets Act in 2020 introduced a new disclosure
requirement. Large shareholders holding more
than 5% of a firm’s shares must now disclose
changes in their investment purpose, including
a shift to ‘general investment’, which explicitly
permits active shareholder engagement. This
reform provides a rare opportunity to observe the
timing and targets of ESG shareholder engagement
in an otherwise opaque setting.
To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has
empirically examined the effect of ESG shareholder
engagement on firm value using a comprehensive
panel dataset in the Korean capital market while
explicitly accounting for the role of corporate gov­
ernance as a conditioning factor. Addressing this
gap, this study investigates whether ESG share­
holder engagement by an influential institutional
investor enhances firm value and whether this
effect depends on firms’ internal corporate govern­
ance quality.
Accordingly, this study addresses the following
research questions.
First, does ESG shareholder engagement by an
influential institutional investor enhance firm
value in the Korean capital market?
Second, does the effect of ESG shareholder
engagement on firm value depend on firms’ inter­
nal corporate governance quality?
Drawing on stakeholder theory, we argue that
ESG shareholder engagement can influence man­
agerial decision-making by increasing managerial
attention to ESG-related risks and stakeholder
expectations, thereby contributing to firm value
creation. Using panel data on 3104 firm-year
observations from Korean listed companies on
the KOSPI and KOSDAQ between 2020 and
2023, we employ fixed-effects OLS regressions to
examine the relationship between NPS ESG share­
holder engagement and firm value, measured by
the book-to-market ratio.
This study contributes to theory in several
important ways. First, by focusing on ESG share­
holder engagement rather than ESG performance,
this study extends stakeholder theory by highlight­
ing the role of institutional investors as active gov­
ernance agents who shape managerial attention to

the corporate governance literature by demonstrat­

a substitute external governance mechanism, parti­

weak. Third, by providing evidence from a market
dominated by a single influential public pension
fund, this study enriches institutional perspectives

II. Literature review and hypothesis
development
Review of previous studies

A growing body of literature examines ESG share­
holder engagement as a distinct form of investor
activism through which institutional investors seek
to influence firms’ environmental, social, and gov­
ernance practices. Prior studies document that
institutional investors engage firms on ESG issues
through private dialogues, voting, and shareholder
proposals, particularly when ESG issues are finan­
cially material and investors possess sufficient own­
ership stakes and influence (Barko, Cremers, and
Renneboog 2022; Dimson, Karakas, and Li 2015).
Empirical evidence suggests that ESG shareholder
engagement can lead to improvements in firms’ ESG
performance. For example, Akey and Appel (2019)
and Naaraayanan, Sachdeva, and Sharma (2021)
show that institutional investor engagement reduces
environmental emissions of target firms. Barko,
Cremers, and Renneboog (2022) and Bauer,
Derwall, and Tissen (2023) further document that
ESG engagement is associated with improvements in
ESG ratings and reductions in greenhouse gas emis­
sions. These findings indicate that institutional
investors can actively shape firms’ ESG practices
rather than merely selecting firms with strong ESG
performance.

Another extensive strand of literature investi­
gates the relationship between ESG performance
and firm value. While early studies viewed ESG
activities as a cost that could reduce shareholder
value (Friedman 2007; Vance 1975), more recent
research suggests that ESG performance can
enhance firm value through improved risk man­
agement, stakeholder trust, and access to capital
(Fatemi and Fooladi 2013; Fatemi, Glaum, and
Kaiser 2018).
Event-based studies show that negative ESG
events lead to significant stock price declines,
whereas positive ESG events generate limited
upside, highlighting the insurance-like role of
ESG in mitigating downside risk (Krüger 2015).
Meta-analytic
evidence
further
supports
a generally positive association between ESG per­
formance and financial performance, albeit with
substantial heterogeneity across institutional con­
texts (Atz et al. 2023; Bai and Kim 2024; Friede,
Busch, and Bassen 2015).
More recent studies directly examine the rela­
tionship between ESG shareholder engagement
and firm value. Dimson, Karakas, and Li (2015)
provide seminal evidence that successful ESG
engagements generate positive abnormal returns
and improvements in operating performance.
Barko, Cremers, and Renneboog (2022) and
Bauer, Derwall, and Tissen (2023) find that firms
targeted by ESG engagement experience excess
stock returns and improved profitability. Hoepner
et al. (2024) further show that ESG engagement
reduces downside risk, suggesting that engagement
can enhance firm value through both return
enhancement and risk mitigation.
Despite these findings, most existing studies
focus on developed Western markets and primarily

estimate average effects, leaving open questions
regarding the conditions under which ESG share­
holder engagement is most effective.
Prior literature suggests that ESG shareholder
engagement may influence firm value through
multiple interrelated channels, as illustrated in
Figure 1. First, engagement can improve firms’
ESG performance by encouraging better manage­
ment of environmental and social risks and gov­
ernance practices. Improved ESG performance, in
turn, can enhance operational performance
through higher customer loyalty, improved
employee productivity, and more efficient resource
allocation (Albuquerque, Koskinen, and Zhang
2019; Edmans 2011, 2012).
Second, ESG shareholder engagement may affect
firm value through market-based channels. By sig­
nalling improved ESG management and reduced
risk exposure, engagement can increase investor
demand and lower perceived risk, leading to higher
stock prices and reduced downside risk (Hoepner
et al. 2024; Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo 2017).
Together, these operational and market channels
suggest a positive link between ESG shareholder
engagement and firm value.

Theoretical framework and hypothesis
development

This study draws on stakeholder theory to develop
hypotheses regarding the relationship between ESG
shareholder engagement and firm value.
Stakeholder theory posits that firms create value
by managing relationships with stakeholders who
provide critical resources, including customers,
employees, investors, and regulators (Freeman
1984). From the managerial perspective of

stakeholder theory, firms are expected to prioritize
stakeholders who control key resources essential
for long-term value creation (Fernando and
Lawrence 2014).
From this perspective, ESG shareholder engage­
ment by institutional investors can be viewed as an
external governance mechanism that increases
managerial attention to stakeholder-related risks
and expectations. By engaging firms on ESG issues,
institutional investors can encourage managers to
adopt strategies that enhance long-term value by
mitigating risks and strengthening stakeholder
relationships.
Pro arguments suggest that ESG shareholder
engagement enhances firm value. Engagement can
improve ESG performance, reduce regulatory and
reputational risks, and strengthen relationships
with customers and employees, thereby improving
operational performance and market valuation
(Barko, Cremers, and Renneboog 2022; Baron
2008; Dimson, Karakas, and Li 2015).
Contra arguments, however, caution that ESG
shareholder engagement may not always create
value. Engagement can impose compliance costs,
divert managerial attention from core business
activities, or lead to symbolic ESG actions without
substantive improvements. Moreover, powerful
institutional investors may pursue ESG agendas
that reflect their own preferences rather than valuemaximizing objectives, potentially leading to man­
agerial entrenchment or inefficient resource alloca­
tion (Bénabou and Tirole 2006; Krüger 2015).
Given these competing arguments, the net effect
of ESG shareholder engagement on firm value
remains an empirical question that requires careful
investigation.
Based on the above discussion, we propose the
following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1 (H1): ESG shareholder engagement
by institutional investors is associated with firm
value.
Corporate governance, in principle, addresses the
relationships and structures among the board of
directors, shareholders, and executives to mitigate
agency problems arising from the separation of
ownership and control (Berle and Means 2017).
By
implementing

governance

mechanisms – such as executive compensation
packages and board oversight – firms can align
managerial and shareholder interests while redu­
cing agency costs (Daily, Dalton, and Cannella
2003). In recent years, the scope of corporate gov­
ernance has expanded to encompass the rights and
responsibilities of various stakeholders that influ­
ence corporate decision-making (Aoki 2001).
Effective corporate governance can enhance not
only shareholder value but also the interests of
other stakeholders (Brickley and Zimmerman
2010). Fuenzalida et al. (2013) emphasized that
good corporate governance attracts capital, ensures
the proper management of firms, protects the
rights of investors and other stakeholders, builds
trust in financial markets, and enhances
competitiveness.
Research has explored the impact of corporate
governance on ESG performance, with many stu­
dies highlighting a positive relationship.
Compensation packages linked to long-term firm
value can incentivize executives to enhance firm
value through ESG management (Conyon 2006).
Cassely et al. (2020) found that firms with strong
corporate governance are more likely to implement
effective ESG strategies, improving ESG perfor­
mance and creating shareholder value. Nirino
et al. (2022) emphasized the importance of board
decisions in achieving ESG goals and noted that
ESG issues could harm a firm’s reputation and
market share. According to Ingley and Van Der
Walt (2004), as the need for institutional investors
to monitor corporate governance has been increas­
ingly emphasized, they are expected to take an
active role in influencing their portfolio firms on
behalf of their stakeholders. Institutional investors
have incentives to oversee and influence investee
companies’ internal governance structures to
reduce agency costs and protect shareholder
value. From the perspective that institutional inves­
tors constitute an external corporate governance
mechanism, they can serve as a complement to
internal governance structures (McCahery,
Sautner, and Starks 2016).
As discussed above, prior studies suggest that
ESG shareholder engagement can influence firms’
operational and market outcomes through
improved ESG management and risk mitigation.
However, these effects are unlikely to be uniform

governance conditions. This heterogeneity moti­

III. Data and methodology
Sample and data

This study aims, first, to analyse the impact of
institutional investors’ ESG shareholder engage­
ment on firm value, and second, to examine how
corporate governance influences the relationship
between ESG shareholder engagement and firm
value. To achieve this, the study selected manufac­
turing firms listed on the KOSPI and KOSDAQ,
which closed their financial year at the end of
December, from 2020 to 2023, as the research sam­
ple. Consistent with prior studies, firms in the
financial sector are excluded due to their distinct
regulatory environment and accounting practices,
which may confound comparisons of firm value
and financial performance (Benlemlih, Jaballah,
and Peillex 2018; Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo
2017). Firms with fiscal year-ends other than
December are also excluded to ensure

gate potential seasonality effects (Choi and Park
2019; Watanabe et al. 2013). Furthermore, firms
lacking corporate governance and financial data
were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 3104
firm-year observations.
Engagement data

To reap the benefits of effective engagement, insti­
tutional investors’ ESG shareholder engagement is
typically conducted privately, limiting access to
information about which companies, when, and
on what issues they engage in (Bauer, Clark, and
Viehs 2013; Gifford 2010; Gillan and Starks 2003;
Logsdon and Van Buren 2009).
In February 2020, the ‘Enforcement Decree of
the Financial Investment Services and Capital
Markets Act’ in South Korea was amended, intro­
ducing a reform to the large shareholder reporting
system. This reform has facilitated the partial
acquisition of data on the timing and target com­
panies of institutional investors’ ESG shareholder
engagement.
This system requires investors who are holding
more than 5% of a listed company’s shares to dis­
close their holdings status and the objectives of
holding stocks when there is a change of holdings
or the objectives of holding. Prior to the amend­
ment, the objectives of holding stocks were divided
into two categories: ‘investment purposes only’ and
‘for affecting the management rights of an issuer’.
Under the revised system, a new category, ‘general
investment’, was introduced. It refers to situations
where the investor does not aim to influence man­
agement but engages in active shareholder activ­
ities, such as shareholder engagement by
institutional investors. According to the revision,
if a large shareholder engages in such activities, the
purpose of ownership must be updated from
‘investment purposes only’ to ‘general investment’
and disclosed within a specified period. For this

reason, if an institutional investor reports a change
in their holding objectives to ‘general investment’,
it indicates an intention of engagement with the
investee company, thereby making it feasible to use
this as proxy data for shareholder engagement.
This study manually collected instances where
large shareholders changed their purpose of hold­
ing stocks to ‘general investment’ from the ‘Report
on Significant Holdings of Stocks, etc’. disclosed on
the Financial Supervisory Service’s electronic dis­
closure system (DART System) from 2020 onward
and used this as a proxy for institutional investors’
ESG shareholder engagement data. However, while
the disclosure allows for the identification of which
investors engaged in shareholder activities with
which companies and when, it does not provide
details on the topics or success of the engagement.
Therefore, this limits the ability to conduct
a detailed analysis of shareholder engagement, as
prior research has done.
As shown in Table 1, seven investors disclosed
a change in holding purpose to ‘general investment’
during 2020 to 2023 period through the electronic
disclosure system, with 217 companies. This study
focuses on the analysis of the impact of ESG share­
holder engagement on firm value, specifically for
companies where the NPS, as an influential institu­
tional investor with substantial holdings in domestic
firms and a clear ESG shareholder engagement pol­
icy, has disclosed a change to ‘general investment’.
Given the NPS’s disclosure of such changes and its
established policy and processes regarding ESG
engagement, this study considers the companies dis­
closed by the NPS as part of its ESG shareholder
engagement data for analysis.
Firm-level data

Financial data was collected from FnGuide’s
‘DataGuide’, while corporate governance data was

obtained from the corporate governance ratings
published annually by the Korea Institute of
Corporate Governance and Sustainability (KCGS).
The KCGS, established in June 2002 as
a subsidiary of the Korea Exchange, has been eval­
uating the corporate governance of listed compa­
nies in Korea since 2003. The KCGS evaluates and
publicly discloses corporate governance ratings on
a seven-point scale annually, utilizing publicly dis­
closed materials, media reports, sustainability
reports, and data from government agencies. The
corporate governance ratings from KCGS are
derived from a comprehensive assessment of
board leadership, shareholder rights protection,
auditing, and stakeholder communication. This
data has been used in numerous previous studies
to represent the corporate governance quality of
Korean companies (Cho and Chun 2016; Lee,
Chung, and Morscheck 2020; Pae and Choi 2011).

Empirical model

This study used panel data to examine the
impact of institutional investors’ ESG share­
holder engagement on firm value, and to
empirically analyse whether corporate govern­
ance moderates the relationship between ESG
shareholder engagement and firm value.
A pooled ordinary least squares (OLS) model
may suffer from omitted variable bias due to
unobserved
firm-specific
characteristics.
Random effects models were also considered;
however, the Hausman test rejects the null
hypothesis that unobserved firm effects are
uncorrelated with the regressors. Accordingly,
a fixed effects model is employed to control for
time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity, consis­
tent with prior ESG and firm value studies
(Benlemlih, Jaballah, and Peillex 2018).
Year and industry fixed effects are included to
control for macroeconomic shocks and industry-

Endogeneity considerations

Potential endogeneity concerns remain. To address
concerns about reverse causality, firm value is mea­
sured one year after ESG shareholder engagement.
Firm fixed effects are included to account for timeinvariant unobserved characteristics that may
jointly affect engagement and firm value, and
a comprehensive set of control variables is
employed to capture observable determinants of
firm valuation. Although these measures may help
mitigate endogeneity concerns, they do not fully
eliminate them. Accordingly, the results should be
interpreted as conditional associations rather than
causal effects.

The moderating role of corporate governance on the
relationship between ESG shareholder engagement
and firm value

Corporate governance can be broadly categorized
into internal governance, which consists of the
board of directors, general shareholders’ meetings,
and management, and external governance, which
includes institutional investors and other stake­
holders. According to the OECD Principles on
Corporate Governance, stakeholders should have
the opportunity to seek effective redress if their
rights are violated, be encouraged to participate in
corporate governance to enhance firm perfor­
mance, and recognize the significant role of insti­
tutional investors in governance. In this context,
institutional investors, as part of the broader cor­
porate governance framework, can complement
internal governance mechanisms.
As discussed in previous studies, firms with
strong corporate governance structures are likely
to have already made significant efforts to enhance
their ESG performance. This suggests that the
impact of institutional investors, as part of the
external governance framework, may be relatively
limited in such firms. This study examines whether
institutional investors can play a complementary
role in firms with weaker corporate governance
and whether corporate governance quality moder­
ates the effect of ESG shareholder engagement on
firm value. Specifically, it investigates whether
firms with weaker corporate governance experi­
ence a stronger impact from institutional investors’
ESG engagement, leading to more effective ESG
implementation. To address this question, H2 is
formulated and tested.

Figure 3. The moderating role of corporate governance.

The results of Model 2 support the moderating
effect of corporate governance on the relationship
between the NPS’s ESG shareholder engagement
and firm value at the 5% significance level. The
regression coefficient of the interaction term is
0.21876, indicating that as the corporate govern­
ance rating increases, the positive relationship
between ENG and BTM weakens. Conversely,
when the corporate governance rating is lower,
this positive relationship is strengthened. The
moderating effect of corporate governance is
more clearly illustrated in Figure 3. Firms with
the lowest corporate governance rating exhibit
a steeper slope compared to those with the highest
rating, indicating that the positive impact of ESG
shareholder engagement on firm value is more
pronounced in firms with weaker corporate gov­
ernance structures.
Contributions

This study makes several important theoretical
contributions to the literature on ESG, shareholder
engagement, and corporate governance.
First, this study advances the ESG literature by
shifting the focus from ESG performance outcomes
to ESG shareholder engagement as a governance
mechanism. While a large body of prior research
examines whether firms with superior ESG perfor­
mance enjoy higher firm value, considerably less
attention has been paid to how investors actively
shape firms’ ESG practices and how such engage­
ment affects firm value. By empirically demonstrat­
ing that ESG shareholder engagement by an
influential institutional investor enhances firm
value, this study provides direct evidence that

APPLIED ECONOMICS

ESG engagement itself – rather than ESG perfor­
mance alone – constitutes a value-relevant
mechanism.
Second, drawing on stakeholder theory, this
study contributes to the theoretical understanding
of how ESG shareholder engagement influences
firm value. Consistent with the managerial per­
spective of stakeholder theory, the findings suggest
that institutional investors’ ESG engagement
increases managerial attention to stakeholderrelated risks and expectations, thereby improving
firms’ ability to manage ESG-related risks and
opportunities. This extends stakeholder theory by
highlighting institutional investors as active gov­
ernance agents who influence managerial decisionmaking beyond traditional monitoring roles.
Third, and most importantly, this study contri­
butes to the corporate governance literature by
identifying corporate governance quality as a key
boundary condition in the ESG engagement – firm
value relationship. The results show that the posi­
tive effect of ESG shareholder engagement on firm
value is significantly stronger when internal corpo­
rate governance quality is weak. This finding pro­
vides novel evidence that ESG shareholder
engagement functions as a substitute external gov­
ernance mechanism, complementing insufficient
internal governance structures. By explicitly mod­
elling this moderating effect, the study moves
beyond average treatment effects and offers
a more nuanced theoretical understanding of
when and where ESG shareholder engagement is
most effective.
Together, these contributions enrich the litera­
ture by integrating ESG shareholder engagement,
stakeholder theory, and corporate governance into
a unified framework that explains how and under
what conditions investor-driven ESG engagement
creates firm value.
V. Conclusion

Following the financial crisis, discussions on insti­
tutional investors’ fiduciary responsibilities have
expanded, particularly regarding their role in mon­
itoring whether investee companies are properly
managing ESG factors that affect financial perfor­
mance. When companies fail to meet ESG stan­
dards, institutional investors engage with them to

13

improve ESG performance and enhance firm value.
This study explores the relationship between ESG
shareholder engagement by the NPS, a major insti­
tutional investor in Korea, and firm value, using
data from companies listed on the KOSPI and
KOSDAQ from 2020 to 2023. An OLS regression
with fixed effects is conducted, including year and
industry dummies. The study also examines
whether corporate governance quality moderates
the ESG engagement-firm value relationship.
The analysis found a significant positive rela­
tionship between the NPS’s ESG engagement and
firm value, especially when corporate governance
quality is low. Based on these findings, this study
draws the following conclusions. First, in the
Korean market, the ESG shareholder engagement
of influential institutional investors can improve
a company’s management of ESG-related risks
and opportunities, thereby enhancing business per­
formance and stock returns, ultimately leading to
an increase in firm value. Second, institutional
investors can monitor corporate ESG practices
from the outside and address significant ESG
issues, thereby playing a role in complementing
the governance structure of companies with weak
internal governance. As such, ESG shareholder
engagement in companies with low-quality corpo­
rate governance may be particularly effective in
enhancing firm value.
This study holds significance as the first to
empirically analyse the impact of the ESG share­
holder engagement of the NPS, a key shareholder
in Korea, on firm value through improvements in
corporate ESG management, based on stakeholder
theory. Additionally, it examines how corporate
governance quality moderates the effect of ESG
shareholder engagement on firm value. The find­
ings reaffirm that institutional investors, beyond
their fiduciary duty, should recognize the necessity
of ESG shareholder engagement from
a profitability perspective as well.
Given that this study focuses on the National
Pension Service and the Korean capital market,
the findings offer several practical and policy
implications.
For institutional investors, the results suggest
that ESG shareholder engagement should be
viewed not only as a fiduciary obligation or ethical
practice, but also as a value-enhancing investment

14

J. LEE AND D. NAM

strategy. Particularly in firms with weak govern­
ance, proactive ESG engagement can improve firm
value and reduce long-term risk exposure.
For corporations, the findings imply that ESG
shareholder engagement by institutional investors
should not be interpreted solely as external pres­
sure. Instead, firms – especially those with weaker
governance – may benefit from engaging construc­
tively with institutional investors to improve ESG
management and long-term value creation.
For policymakers, the study highlights the
importance of institutional frameworks that facil­
itate effective ESG shareholder engagement.
Enhancing ESG disclosure requirements and
ensuring transparency in shareholder engagement
activities can improve the effectiveness of steward­
ship codes and strengthen market discipline. In the
Korean context, policies that support institutional
investors’ engagement activities can serve as an
indirect tool to improve corporate governance
and ESG practices without imposing excessive reg­
ulatory burdens on firms.
This study has several limitations that provide
avenues for future research.
First, due to data constraints, ESG shareholder
engagement is proxied using publicly disclosed
engagement activities by the NPS for firms in
which it holds at least 5% ownership. As most
ESG engagements are conducted privately, the
sample may not capture the full extent of engage­
ment
activities,
potentially
limiting
generalizability.
Second, the relatively short sample period (2020­
–2023) restricts the ability to examine long-term
effects of ESG shareholder engagement. Future
studies could utilize longer time horizons as more
data become available.
Third, this study focuses on firm value as the
primary outcome variable and does not empirically
examine intermediate mechanisms such as changes
in ESG performance or operational efficiency.
Future research could explore these channels
directly to further unpack how ESG shareholder
engagement translates into firm value.
Finally, while this study focuses on a dominant
public pension fund in Korea, future research
could examine whether similar governance substi­
tution effects arise in other institutional settings or
among different types of institutional investors.
